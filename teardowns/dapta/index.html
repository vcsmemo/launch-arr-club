How Dapta Makes Money

Dapta sells an always-on sales rep to the businesses AI forgot — Spanish-speaking SMB owners — and finds them through the founder's own content, not ads.

200,000+Users, adding ~4,000 businesses a week

1. What problem does it solve?

A missed call is a lost customer. For a roofer in Houston, a law firm in Miami, or an HVAC company in Denver, most leads arrive by phone, text, or WhatsApp — and nobody is there to answer. Big companies solve this with a sales staff. A 10-person business can't.

Dapta's agent, Dapti, answers every inbound lead in seconds — by phone, text, or WhatsApp, in English or Spanish — qualifies the caller, and books the appointment straight into the owner's calendar and CRM. No missed calls, no back-and-forth scheduling.

The problem is old (speed-to-lead: contact a lead within 5 minutes and you're ~100x more likely to reach them than after 30). The new part is that an AI agent can now do the answering, and a non-technical owner can set it up by describing what they want in plain language.

2. Who pays, how much, and in what scenario?

The buyer: the owner-operator of a small service business — home services (HVAC, plumbing, cleaning), law firms, insurance brokers, clinics, universities. Plus a long tail of larger Latin American companies (Mercado Libre, Rappi, Siigo) that use it for Spanish-language sales.

Usage is billed in credits, not minutes or calls. Dapta does not publish how many credits a call minute or message costs on its pricing page — a real cost-transparency gap, and something a buyer only learns by trialling it.

The scenario that makes someone pay: the first missed lead that costs a job. A customer tells the story themselves: Aether Mechanical, an HVAC company in Denver, says Dapta helped grow revenue "upwards of 20 percent." Once the first agent works, the business builds the next one — follow-ups, reactivating old leads, client intake, customer service. More than 50 paying customers have expanded their usage 10x or more within their first six months. That is the revenue engine: land with one sales agent, expand into the whole front office.

Sanity check (our estimate, not a reported figure): $4M run rate ÷ ~$99/mo average implies roughly 3,000–4,000 paying customers — about 2% of its 200,000+ users. The free tier is the top of the funnel; conversion is the number that matters, and Dapta has not disclosed it.

3. How did it find these people?

Almost entirely through the founder's own content. From Dapta's seed announcement, in Nico Rojas's own words: "~4,000 new businesses join every week, almost all through our own content."

Teach, don't sell. Rojas built an acquisition engine in Latin America first: teaching business owners AI in Spanish, a free product, and founders with no technical background building their own agents. Daily office hours, weekly webinars, in-person workshops — in the language the customer thinks in.

Language as distribution. Almost every AI platform is sold, taught, and supported in English. Dapta picked the founders who don't buy in English, starting with Spanish speakers in the US. That is not a niche: Latino-owned businesses grew 44% in five years, revenue up 36% (Stanford). The audience is one of the fastest-growing parts of the American economy — and almost nobody was explaining AI to them in Spanish.

A founder with an audience. Rojas started programming at 13, co-founded Imagine Apps in Bogotá at 17, grew it to ~100 employees. He has built a social following of 400,000+ with content about AI for small businesses. Most users arrive through the company's own social channels "rather than paid acquisition."

The same engine crossed over: what worked for Spanish-speaking founders in Latin America worked, almost unchanged, for Spanish-speaking founders in the US.

4. Why does it work?

Dapta's wedge is not the AI — voice agents are becoming a commodity. The wedge is who it serves and how it reaches them: a large, fast-growing, underserved buyer (non-English-speaking SMB owners) reached through trusted teaching in their own language, on the channel they already live on (WhatsApp), with a product that sets itself up (no implementation partner, no onboarding call, no engineers). Enterprise AI sends engineers to every customer; that will never reach a 10-person HVAC company. Dapta built the opposite — and the free tier plus daily teaching turns trust into signups at ~4,000 businesses a week.

5. Could you build it?

The product, yes. The business, not by copying the product.

Replicable: the shape — a no-code voice/text agent on WhatsApp + phone, connected to a calendar and CRM — can be assembled from existing APIs (telephony, speech, LLMs) by a small team. The "describe what you want in plain language and the agent builds itself" loop is the hard product work, but it is well-trodden ground now.

Not replicable by cloning: the distribution. 400,000 followers who trust one founder, two years of daily teaching in Spanish, and 200,000 users' worth of real conversation data. A new entrant would need its own trust channel — a different language, a different trade, a different community — not a better voice agent.

The opening it leaves: credit-based pricing with unpublished per-unit costs is a trust gap. A competitor that prices in plain minutes or booked meetings — and says so on the pricing page — has a clean story to tell.

What we don't know

  • Paid conversion rate (free → paying) and churn — the two numbers that decide whether 200,000 users is a business or a mailing list.
  • True cost per call minute (credits are unpublished), so real gross margin is unknowable from outside.
  • How much of the $4M run rate is the $99–$499 self-serve tail vs. larger contracts (Mercado Libre, Rappi).
