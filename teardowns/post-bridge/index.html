How Post Bridge Makes Money

Post Bridge sells 30 seconds instead of an hour — write once, publish to ten platforms — and it found its buyers by building in public in front of the creators who feel that pain every day.

1. What problem does it solve?

Short-form video is the cheapest distribution a small creator has — and the most repetitive work. To grow his consumer app Curiosity Quench past 100,000 downloads, founder Jack Friks posted short-form content daily. About an hour a day went to it, and roughly half of that hour was pure copying: the same video, pasted by hand into Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, X, and the rest.

The schedulers that existed felt expensive and bloated for the job. They were built around teams — approval flows, shared inboxes, seats, client reporting — while the person doing the posting was one person doing their own marketing. Post Bridge does one job: write once, publish to ten platforms from one dashboard, in about 30 seconds. Per-platform caption overrides, a calendar, bulk upload, and analytics round it out, but the core promise never changed: the hour becomes half a minute.

That is a problem the founder had himself, every day, which is why the first version was narrow on purpose. He mentioned the idea in a September 2024 build log, validated it with mockups before writing much code, and built the first version in roughly a month. It launched in October 2024 with a hard paywall and no free tier — if the tool saved you an hour a day, it was worth paying for from day one.

2. Who pays, how much, and in what scenario?

The buyer: a solo creator, indie founder, or one-person marketing operation who posts the same content across several platforms and is tired of doing it by hand. Not an agency, not an enterprise social team — the pricing page says so outright: the product "is built for one person doing the marketing, not a team."

Pricing is by connected account, not by channel — fifteen accounts can be fifteen TikTok accounts, or ten TikTok and five Instagram, or any mix. Posts are unlimited on paid plans, and existing customers are grandfathered when prices change. The pricing page's own framing is the pitch: one business on every platform fits inside the $39 plan.

The scenario that makes someone pay: the day the manual routine breaks — a video goes out late, a platform gets skipped, or the creator counts the hours. The buyer is usually already posting daily and already feeling the cost; Post Bridge just has to be cheaper than the hour. The founder still answers every support email himself, most within a few hours, which for a solo buyer is part of the product: you talk to Jack, not a ticket queue.

Sanity check (our estimate, not a reported figure): ~$48,500 MRR ÷ ~1,600 subscriptions ≈ $30 per subscription per month — consistent with a $39/mo entry plan once annual discounts and grandfathered early pricing are factored in. And a rough funnel read: ~1,600 paying customers against 29,000+ signups (a September 2026 snapshot) implies roughly 5–6% of signups convert to paying — healthy for a product that launched behind a hard paywall, though the two snapshots are months apart, so treat the ratio as approximate.

3. How did it find these people?

Almost entirely by building in public. Friks had spent years trying to make money online before Post Bridge — YouTube channels, print-on-demand, affiliate blogs, crypto — and has been open that most of it produced modest or temporary results. He only learned to code in earnest in 2023. What changed with Post Bridge is that the audience and the product finally matched: he was already the person posting short-form video every day, and the tool was the thing that made that day shorter. Growth came from X build-in-public posts, the short-form formats he already used for his apps, and word of mouth among creators and founders. Product Hunt and Indie Hackers posts contributed early visibility. He has been explicit that paid acquisition is not the core engine.

Two moments did outsized work. The first was saying no in public: in spring 2025 he turned down a $1 million acquisition offer, noting at the time that the business was probably worth less than that. The attention from declining appeared to help, and the decision kept the business independent — bootstrapped, with no outside investors beyond family support, and Y Combinator turned down as well. The second was timing the AI-agent wave: in 2026 Post Bridge shipped a REST API, an npm CLI, and an MCP server so tools like Claude and ChatGPT can schedule and publish on a user's behalf. It is listed in the Claude connector directory and as a ChatGPT plugin, and after the ChatGPT listing appeared in early September 2026 it set four consecutive daily signup records, well above the prior average of about 50 a day.

The numbers compounded slowly, then fast. Roughly $6,000–$7,000 MRR within the first several months, with 500+ customers — then a plateau, then an eight-month stretch of limited growth after the acquisition offer. By mid-2026 MRR had roughly doubled to around $35,000, and by mid-November 2026 it reached approximately $48,500, with about $582,000 in all-time revenue and the business past $500,000 in annualised revenue.

4. Why does it work?

Post Bridge's wedge is not the scheduler — cross-posting is a commodity, and Buffer and Hootsuite have sold it for a decade. The wedge is who it is for and how it is priced: one person, not a team; accounts, not seats; unlimited posts, not usage tiers. Incumbents charge for the team features a solo creator never uses. Post Bridge charges $39 for the one job, and the founder's own public numbers do the selling — publishing the revenue, good weeks and flat ones, built the trust that a $39/mo tool from a stranger needs. Then the agent wave handed it a second act for free: the same "publish once" job, now done by Claude or ChatGPT on the user's behalf, through an API and MCP server the incumbents were slower to ship.

Underneath, the economics are what make a solo business viable at this size. Infrastructure — Supabase, Vercel, Trigger.dev, Unkey, the X API, and smaller items — totals under $2,000 a month against $35,000+ MRR, implying gross margins above 90% and net margins in the high 80s after payment fees. One operator, no hires, no office, no ad budget: nearly every dollar of the ~$48,500 MRR is margin, which is why a business of 1,600 customers can support a founder outright.

5. Could you build it?

The product, yes. The business, not by copying the product.

Replicable: a cross-posting scheduler is well-trodden ground — ten platform APIs, a queue, a calendar, and caption overrides are a small team's work, and the agent interfaces (REST API, MCP server) are now standard patterns. The pricing shape is copyable too: charge per connected account, unlimited posts, keep the surface area small.

Not replicable by cloning: the distribution. Two years of building in public in front of exactly the creators who feel this pain, a public revenue record that turns transparency into trust, and a founder who is himself the target customer — still answering support, still posting daily. A new entrant would need its own trust channel with the same audience, not a better scheduler.

The opening it leaves: ten platform APIs are ten relationships that can break — policy changes, price hikes (the X API is already a line item in the infra bill), or a platform deciding schedulers are competition. And the agent wave cuts both ways: if ChatGPT and Claude become the posting surface, the scheduler risks becoming invisible plumbing. The defence so far is the same as the wedge — being the simple, cheap, founder-backed default for one person doing their own marketing.

What we don't know

  • Churn and retention — the two numbers that decide whether 1,600 subscriptions is a compounding base or a leaky bucket. Neither has been disclosed.
  • Monthly vs. annual plan mix — how much of the ~$48,500 MRR is prepaid annual revenue, which would flatter the monthly figure.
  • Whether the September 2026 signup spike from the ChatGPT listing converted to paying customers at the same rate as organic signups, or mostly stayed free.
  • Whether infrastructure stays under $2,000 a month as volume grows — media storage and API usage both scale with posts, and the X API is a cost that can move.
  • A note on the numbers: all-time revenue (~$582,000) and the annualised run rate (~$48,500 × 12 ≈ $582,000) happen to land on nearly the same figure. They are different numbers — cumulative vs. annualised — and the coincidence is just that.
