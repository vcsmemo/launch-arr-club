How DataFast Makes Money

DataFast sells one answer — which marketing channels bring paying customers — and it found its buyers among the founders who already bought the founder's earlier products. The audience came first; the analytics second.

1. What problem does it solve?

Google Analytics tells you how many visitors you got. It does not tell you which ones paid. A founder who runs ads, posts content, and sponsors newsletters ends up with the same question every month: which of this actually made money?

DataFast answers that one question and stops there. Install the script in under a minute, connect Stripe, LemonSqueezy, Polar, or Shopify, and it attributes revenue to the marketing channels that produced it — revenue per visitor, not traffic for its own sake. Goals, funnels, visitor journeys, and live visitor intelligence sit on top of that idea, plus X link attribution and social media mentions for the founders who live on social. There is also an API, a CLI, and an MCP server, so an AI assistant can query the numbers in plain English.

The pitch is written for a founder who just launched something and wonders which half of the effort worked. The product's own homepage states the contrast plainly: most analytics tools show vanity metrics — pageviews, visitors, bounce rates — and none of them say where the money came from.

2. Who pays, how much, and in what scenario?

The buyer: a solo founder or small SaaS team spending their own money on marketing — the same person who writes the posts, buys the ads, and checks the Stripe dashboard at night.

Pricing verified from DataFast's own homepage, checked Nov 17, 2026. Both plans include the API, CLI and MCP access, social media mentions, and X link attribution. Pricing scales with monthly event volume — the homepage slider sets the volume (10k, 100k, 200k, 1M+ events) — and there is a 14-day free trial with no card required, plus two months free on yearly billing.

The unusual part is what you pay for: events, not seats. A solo founder with a busy site can outpay a 30-person team on the cheaper plan, because the meter runs on traffic, not headcount.

The scenario that makes someone pay: the first month a founder sees that the channel which looked best on clicks produced no revenue, while a quiet one produced the sales. At that point $9 a month is not an analytics subscription; it is the answer to the question the founder was already losing sleep over. And once the script is installed, it keeps answering every month — the retention is built into the install, not into a contract.

Sanity check (our estimate, not a reported figure): his January 2026 newsletter, as summarized by atticusli.com, put DataFast at roughly 1,000 paying customers on $15,800 MRR — about $15.80 per customer per month. At $30,000 MRR on the same math, that implies roughly 1,900 paying customers. Against 28,350 users, that works out to roughly 7% conversion from user to paying customer. Treat it as a rough shape, not a fact: the company has not disclosed the current customer count. One metric warning: October's $31,103 is a monthly revenue line on the founder's income card, not labelled MRR; November is separately described as $30,000 MRR. The two numbers are close but they are not the same metric, so the October line should not be annualized into an ARR claim. Our arithmetic — $30,000 MRR × 12 — implies roughly a $360K annualized run rate, which is our calculation, not a reported figure.

3. How did it find these people?

Three moves, all of them cheap — and the first two are the same move.

The audience arrived before the product did. Marc Lou builds in public in front of 127,000+ followers on X; the homepage says so in his own words. Every feature, fix, and revenue number becomes a post, and every post reaches founders with exactly the problem DataFast solves. The monthly income card is itself the marketing: the October 31, 2026 post drew about 197,000 impressions, 1,348 likes, 202 replies, and 144 quote posts within eight hours. The revenue report is the ad.

The funnel from earlier products. DataFast's first wave of paying customers came directly from people who had already bought ShipFast or CodeFast — the same funnel, one stage later. The site's footer still carries a "Built with ShipFast" badge, cross-linking the portfolio in plain view. A founder who bought the boilerplate to launch a SaaS is the exact person who next needs to know whether the launch worked.

A launch the market noticed, then an affiliate engine. DataFast launched in October 2024, reached roughly $300 MRR within two weeks, and was named #1 Product of the Day on Product Hunt on January 28, 2026 — #2 Product of the Week and #4 of the month as well. The homepage also offers a 50% commission for up to 12 months to affiliates, turning the same founder audience into a sales channel. The growth since is steady rather than spiky: roughly $21,000 a month by late 2025, around $27,000 a month by mid-2026, $30,000 MRR by November 2026.

4. Why does it work?

DataFast's wedge is not the analytics — a tracking script is a commodity, and Google Analytics is free. The wedge is the question, asked to the right buyer, by a founder that buyer already trusts: founders spending their own money want to know which channel paid, and the person selling them the answer is the same person whose revenue posts they have been reading for years. The product is also the durable half of its founder's portfolio: the one-time ShipFast boilerplate peaked at $135,000 a month in 2024 and settled to about $3,537 a month (and $1,694 on the October income card), while DataFast's subscriptions compounded from roughly $300 MRR at launch to $30,000 MRR two years later. One-time sales spike and decay; a subscription that answers a question founders ask every month compounds instead.

5. Could you build it?

The product, yes. The business, not by copying the product.

Replicable: the shape — a lightweight tracking script, payment-provider attribution, goals and funnels, an API and MCP server — can be assembled from existing APIs by a small team. The pieces are commodity, and the product itself shows how far one person can get in two years.

Not replicable by cloning: the distribution. 127,000 followers who trust one founder, a buyer list from two earlier products, a #1 Product Hunt launch, and 28,350 users' worth of behavioural data. A clone would need its own trust channel — a different audience that has been listened to first — not a better dashboard.

The opening it leaves: event-based pricing punishes traffic spikes, so a heavy-traffic site pays more for the same answer; Google Analytics is free and Plausible and Simple Analytics compete on privacy and simplicity; there are no heatmaps or scroll-depth tracking; and data import from Google Analytics is still listed as "next" — Plausible import only for now. And the risk the company carries is the one every audience-built business carries: the whole funnel is one person's X account.

What we don't know

  • The exact paying-customer count now — our ~1,900 is an estimate from the January 2026 ratio, not a reported figure.
  • Churn now — the January 2026 newsletter summary put it at 7%; the current figure is undisclosed.
  • How much of the $31,103 October line is subscription MRR versus annual prepayments — the card labels it a monthly revenue line, not MRR, and November is separately described as $30,000 MRR.
  • The affiliate channel's share of signups — a 50% commission is offered, but volume is undisclosed.
  • The monthly/annual plan split, and how much revenue sits on the $9 Starter versus higher event volumes.
  • Whether the $30,000 MRR held past November 2026.
  • One small discrepancy: the homepage says Marc has built 24 startups and earned $2M+ online, while his November 2026 newsletter, as cited in our founder story, puts it at 36 startups and $3M earned. The homepage copy looks older; we use neither figure as a headline number.
