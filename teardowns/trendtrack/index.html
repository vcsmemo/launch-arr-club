How Trendtrack Makes Money

Trendtrack sells the answer to one question — what's my competitor doing right now? — and it found its buyers by giving the data away and charging only for the moment you look closely at one rival.

1. What problem does it solve?

E-commerce runs on imitation, and imitation runs on information. A dropshipper picking a product, a DTC brand refreshing a tired ad, an agency pitching a new client — all of them need to know the same thing: what is actually working, right now, for the stores already winning in their niche. The ad libraries are public, but public is not the same as usable. Meta's ad library shows you ads; it does not tell you which ones have been running for 186 days, which landing page they point to, or which email flow follows the click.

Trendtrack stitches that picture together. Winning ads across Meta, TikTok and Google, fast-growing Shopify stores, high-spending advertisers, brand email flows — refreshed every 24 hours, with 2 to 3 million new ads indexed a day. The pitch, in the company's own words, is that most tools show what worked last month; Trendtrack shows what is working today.

The problem is old — spying on competitors predates the internet — but ad creative now burns out in weeks, not quarters, so "what worked" decays fast. Freshness is not a feature here. It is the product.

2. Who pays, how much, and in what scenario?

The buyer: anyone whose own money rides on an ad decision — a dropshipper validating a product before spending a dollar, a DTC operator whose ads are burning out and needs fresh angles, a brand or agency tracking market shifts before they peak. The homepage's wall of love is founders of nine-figure brand holdings and performance agencies, but the price points say the core buyer is smaller: the solo operator and the small team.

PlanPriceWhat you get
Free$0Browse the whole database — stores, ads, products, mail — without paying
Starter$49/mo5 brand lookups a week, 2 tracked brands
Pro$89/mo30 lookups a week, 10 tracked brands, API/MCP access
Business$159/moUnlimited lookups, 20 tracked brands, API, 3 seats included
EnterpriseCustomUnlimited users, custom API contract, dedicated Slack channel

The unit of value is the lookup: unlocking one brand — its shop analytics, ads, landing pages and emails — for 24 hours. A brand followed in Brandtracker stays permanently unlocked without using a lookup. Everything else is free to browse. That is the whole inversion: the data is the demo, the unlock is the product.

The scenario that makes someone pay: the moment a specific rival matters. A free user finds a store that is scaling, wants the full playbook behind it — which ads, which angles, which landing pages, which emails — and five lookups a week stops being enough. The purchase decision is not "is this database good" but "is $49 a month cheaper than launching one blind ad set" — and against a single wasted $500 test campaign, it is not a close call. Agencies hit the same wall faster: tracking ten competitors permanently is a Pro-plan job, and twenty is Business.

Sanity check (our estimate, not a reported figure): $1M MRR at a blended ~$89 a month (the Pro plan) implies roughly 11,000 paying customers — about 5–6% of the 200,000+ users the homepage claims. At the extremes, all-Starter would mean ~20,000 payers and all-Business ~6,300. Two caveats: the site is currently advertising up to 50% off annual plans, so prepayments lower the effective average, and Enterprise pulls it the other way. The range, not the point estimate, is the honest number.

3. How did it find these people?

Mostly through the founder, in the open, in the buyers' own language. Vincent Alonzi came up through dropshipping, built Digifeel — a review tool for online stores — and started Trendtrack in late 2024, launching publicly in spring 2025. He documents the climb where the buyers live: milestone posts on X in English, a French YouTube channel and Instagram, and constant podcast appearances in French. The October 20 post that announced $900,000 MRR opened with a provocation — "We hit $900K MRR by doing the opposite of every SaaS advice you've read" — and its first hack was the business model itself: "Don't chase paying users. Chase free users. Most SaaS hide everything behind a paywall, so nobody feels the value before paying. On Trendtrack, our data is free." Every milestone post is itself a piece of distribution: the November 9 post announcing $1 million MRR ends "Now let's scale to $10M MRR" — a goal and an ad in the same sentence.

The free tier does the rest of the work. Anyone can browse the store database, the ad libraries and the brand profiles without an account standing between them and the value, which is why the user count can be 200,000 while the paying count is an order of magnitude smaller. A November profile of the company credits influencer marketing, SEO and affiliation as major contributors, claiming more than €100,000 of MRR from SEO and about €3 million via affiliation — but that is the company's own framing, not an audited figure. What is verifiable is the shape: the founder posts, does podcasts constantly, and people talk about the product on Reddit and LinkedIn, with no visible paid-acquisition footprint.

The posted curve: €3 million ARR in under a year (March 2026, via a French tech podcast), $4 million ARR on May 27, $8 million on August 26, $900,000 MRR on October 20, $1 million MRR on November 9 — eighteen months from public launch to a million a month, posted as it happened.

4. Why does it work?

Trendtrack's wedge is not the data — ad libraries are public, and anyone can open Meta's ad library for free. The wedge is freshness plus the unit of sale. Two to three million new ads a day, brand profiles refreshed every 24 hours, and a paywall that sits on exactly one action: looking closely at one rival, right now. A spy tool is only as good as its last refresh, so the refresh is the product — and the free tier proves it on every visit, because the buyer can see the data is current before paying a cent.

Three details make the wedge hold. First, the buyer is spending their own money on ads, so the willingness to pay tracks the cost of a wrong decision, not the cost of the software — one blind test campaign costs more than a month of Pro. Second, the lookup turns a subscription into a metered habit: five lookups a week is enough to taste the product and not enough to run a business on, so the free tier converts by rationing the exact thing the buyer wants most. Third, the audience is built in the language of the buyers — French for the home market, English for the global one — in a niche where most tools are sold in English to people who think in other languages.

It is also, unusually, a business with no venture money behind it: bootstrapped, about ten people, based in Dubai, never raised. There is no burn rate to feed and no growth-at-all-costs board to satisfy — but a data business refreshing millions of ads a day has real infrastructure costs, and the free tier is ~95% of the users generating them.

5. Could you build it?

The product, yes. The business, not by copying the product.

Replicable: the shape — crawl public ad libraries and store data, index it, refresh it daily, sell lookups — can be assembled by a small team. The raw inputs are public: ad spend from Meta's own transparency data, store traffic from SimilarWeb. The genuinely hard product work is the pipeline: crawling at the scale of millions of ads a day, keeping it fresh, and keeping the cost of serving 200,000 free users below the revenue from the ~5% who pay.

Not replicable by cloning: the distribution and the habit. A founder with an audience in the exact niche, two years of daily teaching in French and English, and 200,000 users whose daily check-in is the product's retention loop. A new entrant would need its own trust channel — a different niche, a different language, a different community — not a better ad index.

The opening it leaves: the data itself is public, so the moat is freshness, aggregation and habit — all of which are weakest at the edges. A competitor that goes deep where Trendtrack is broad — one platform, one geography, one vertical with its own data sources — can out-fresh the generalist there. And the lookup model has a trust gap of its own: the buyer never sees how much of a brand's "winning" status is spend, duration, or Trendtrack's own ranking. A tool that shows its work has a clean story to tell.

What we don't know

  • Paying customer count and plan mix — the two numbers that decide whether $1M MRR is 6,000 Business subscribers or 20,000 Starter ones. The founder has not disclosed either.
  • Churn and trial-to-paid conversion — the numbers that decide whether the 200,000 free users are a funnel or a cost center. Free-tier economics at this scale are the whole question.
  • Gross margin after data and infrastructure costs — crawling and refreshing millions of ads daily is not free, and neither is serving 200,000 free users.
  • How much of the $1M MRR is annual prepayments (the site is currently advertising up to 50% off annual plans) versus true monthly recurring revenue, and how much is Enterprise.
  • How much growth actually comes from SEO and affiliates — the €100,000+ MRR from SEO and ~€3M via affiliation figures are company-framed, not independently verified.
  • Team costs and profitability — ten people in Dubai, no funding, but no public numbers on burn or profit.
