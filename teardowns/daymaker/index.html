How Daymaker Makes Money

Daymaker sells the meeting, not the cake — a $85 physical object that does what a thousand cold emails can't, found through one viral post and a founder who has been selling since he was twelve.

1. What problem does it solve?

Cold email is dead as a channel and alive as a habit. Reply rates hover around 1%, and every sales team knows it — but they keep sending, because the alternative (a human doing real research on each prospect) doesn't scale.

Daymaker's answer is deliberately low-tech: instead of another email, send the prospect a cake with your pitch printed on top. The pitch arrives as a physical object on their desk, photographed and shared, impossible to ignore and hard to delete. The company calls it "cold caking."

The AI part is the research, not the cake. For each prospect, Daymaker reads up to 800 public sources — LinkedIn, X, GitHub, press, talks — and builds a cited profile in about 27 minutes, then weighs three gift options and picks the one that fits. The cake was the first gift because it is cheap, fast, and photogenic; the November 2026 relaunch widens the same research engine to any gift, from a framed share certificate to a LEGO set, hand-delivered in 44 cities across 9 countries.

The problem is old (getting a stranger's attention) and the insight is older still: founder William Lindholm's homepage retells the story of Joseph Duveen, the art dealer who sold paintings to America's richest men by knowing, before every meeting, what each man had bought last and what his wife had admired at dinner. "His rivals thought he was lucky." Daymaker is Duveen's homework, automated.

2. Who pays, how much, and in what scenario?

The buyer: a sales team or a founder raising money — anyone whose job depends on getting a meeting with a specific person who ignores email. Early customers include startups (Librar Labs, Archy, Mandalo) and, by the November relaunch, revenue teams at Monaco, Nowadays, Rippling, Salesforce, Serval and PostHog.

The pricing: there is no subscription and no pricing page with tiers. Daymaker charges around $85 per cake, including delivery and the photo on top (Inc., April 2026). Campaigns are priced per person with a budget cap the buyer sets; the homepage demo shows 3 gifts for $244 including delivery. This is transactional revenue, not MRR — every dollar has to be re-earned with the next campaign. Do not annualize it into ARR.

The scenario that makes someone pay: the first meeting that wouldn't have happened otherwise. Mandalo founder Andrew Anderson was fundraising; Daymaker sent his pitch, printed on cakes, to seven VCs. He booked five meetings — and the post about it blew up on social media. Librar Labs started with 20 cakes, saw it work, ordered 100 more, and planned quarterly campaigns for all its customers. Archy, a dental software company, jumped straight to 1,000 cakes after hearing about Daymaker — easier, Lindholm says, than coordinating hundreds of pizzas by itself.

Sanity check (our estimate, not a reported figure): $130,000 a month ÷ ~$85 a cake implies roughly 1,500 cakes a month. That is the number to hold in mind — and it does not reconcile cleanly with the same Inc. piece's "around 200 cakes sold" (see What we don't know). The Side Hustle Show, covering the business in mid-2026, put it at around $4,000 a day in revenue at roughly 30% margins — about $1,000 a day in profit, or ~$39,000 a month on $130K of revenue. A 30% gross margin on a physical product with local-bakery fulfilment is the number that decides whether this is a business or a stunt.

3. How did it find these people?

Almost entirely through one viral moment, amplified by a founder who treats selling as the product.

The post that did the work. The Mandalo story — seven cakes, five VC meetings — "blew up on social media," in Lindholm's words. "Ever since that, it's been crazy. We have so much inbound, we don't know what to do with it." By April, Daymaker had completed 10 pitch campaigns and had 50 companies lined up next. No paid acquisition is mentioned anywhere; the product is its own demo, because every cake arrives photographed.

A founder who has been selling since he was twelve. Lindholm started in sales at 12. In 2025 he won third place in a national competition by booking 17 meetings in four hours — including one with KPMG — on the back of automating cake deliveries. He did not learn sales for this company; the company is the sales skill, productized.

Cold-calling with a free cake as the hook. Before the pivot, the birthday-cake version grew to about $10,000 a month by cold-calling Norwegian companies and offering a free cake in exchange for a meeting. The same motion — a free physical object in exchange for attention — became the product. He bootstrapped the launch with $60,000 earned building live website prototypes for newly registered Norwegian companies on cold calls, using Lovable, telling prospects on the call that he was using AI and finishing the site in 20 minutes after hanging up.

The funnel, in short: a founder who can sell, a product that photographs itself, and one post that proved it in public.

4. Why does it work?

Daymaker's wedge is not the AI — reading 800 sources about a prospect is a feature, and features get copied. The wedge is the unit economics of attention: a $85 object that converts at 35% where email converts at ~1%, sold to people for whom one meeting is worth thousands. The buyer is not buying cake or research; they are buying a booked meeting with a named person, and the price is trivial against the value of that meeting. The pivot is the proof: the same cake, the same delivery network, the same founder — but selling to HR departments (birthday cakes for employees, "too much work and too little output") stalled at $10K a month, while selling to sales teams (meetings) grew 13x in six weeks. The product didn't change. The buyer did.

5. Could you build it?

The product, yes. The business, not by copying the product.

Replicable: the shape — prospect research from public sources, a gift picked from the research, local fulfilment, hand delivery — can be assembled from existing APIs and a network of local vendors by a small team. Lindholm himself built the first version with Lovable despite having no technical background. The research agent is the hard product work, but it is well-trodden ground now.

Not replicable by cloning: the distribution. One viral proof point (seven cakes, five meetings), a founder with a decade of selling behind him at age 20, and the inbound that followed. A new entrant would need its own proof — a different buyer, a different gift, a different moment — not a better research agent.

The opening it leaves: the margin. ~30% on a physical, hand-delivered product leaves little room for error, and the November relaunch's move to higher-value gifts (a framed share, a LEGO set) reads as a search for bigger tickets. A competitor that owns one gift category completely, or one city deeply, still has room — and so does anyone who can make the same meeting happen for less than $85.

What we don't know

  • The cake arithmetic doesn't reconcile. At ~$85 a cake, $130K a month implies ~1,500 cakes a month — but the same Inc. piece says "around 200 cakes sold." One of the two numbers must be dated differently (200 may count only early pitch campaigns, or enterprise orders may price well above $85). We report both as published and flag the tension rather than resolving it.
  • Current revenue (Nov 2026) — the latest revenue figure is April 2026, seven months old. The November relaunch post says customers spent $100K+ on cakes in the prior two weeks — but that is customer spend, not Daymaker revenue, and we do not annualize it or call it MRR.
  • What "monthly revenue" is made of — how much is one-off campaign fees vs. repeat quarterly campaigns (Librar Labs' planned quarterly cadence suggests repeat, but no split is disclosed).
  • Churn and repeat rate — how many of the first 50 customers came back, and how many campaigns the average customer runs.
  • True fulfilment cost — bakery, delivery and research costs per cake beyond the ~30% margin figure, and whether the margin holds as gifts get more expensive than cakes.
  • The $150K ARR figure in some roundups belongs to the old birthday-cake business, not to cold caking. It is not Daymaker's ARR and we do not use it.
