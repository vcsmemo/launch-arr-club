The 21-Year-Old Who Decided Email Itself Was the Problem

Melvin Hagberg joined Y Combinator at 21 to build a customer-support tool for email, then realized the tool solved his own frustration better than anyone else's. Two years later Scape has $3.2M from Y Combinator, General Catalyst and FundersClub — and a YC partner who says users never go back to Gmail.

An interface that hadn't changed in 25 years

More than a billion professionals spend most of their workday in email, inside an interface that, as Scape's launch coverage put it, had not fundamentally changed in 25 years. Answering a single client email could mean reading old threads for context, checking meeting notes for what was agreed, filling out an attached form in a separate tool, uploading it, writing the reply, sending it — and then watching for the answer among dozens of other threads. Deciding what to say was only a fraction of the time. The rest was work about work.

That gap is what Scape was built to close. The Stockholm startup, which emerged from stealth in July 2026, makes an intelligent email inbox that works proactively: it surfaces only the emails that need attention and prepares drafted responses — attachments created or edited included — ready to review and send. On the surface it looks like the inbox you already know. Underneath, it uses context from your email, meetings, calendar and attachments to keep you focused on what actually matters.

A support tool that solved its founder's own problem

Melvin Hagberg founded Scape while participating in Y Combinator's summer 2024 batch, as a 21-year-old solo founder. The company did not start as an inbox at all. It started as a customer-support tool for email — software that helped support teams work through their queues faster.

The pivot came from Hagberg's own desk. He realized the tool he had built could help solve his own frustration with email better than it served its original market, and he rebuilt the company around that insight. Elis Hodzic, who had originally backed the company as an investor, joined as a co-founder in 2025. The two 23-year-old Swedes now lead a five-person team in Stockholm, hiring across engineering and product.

Hagberg frames the bet plainly: email is where most professionals get their work done, and they do not need another chatbot or Gmail plugin. They need an interface purpose-built for the work that happens in email. The mission, as he puts it, is to let professionals do more impactful work — and to make email fun and fast to use.

The product: drafts before you open the thread

At the core of Scape is the inbox view. Instead of an unread pile, it surfaces only the emails that require attention, organized around custom labels built on how you prioritize work — Customers, Hiring and the like. Lower-priority mail, notifications and newsletters are hidden by default, but never lost.

Open a thread and the work is already prepared. Scape has drafted a response that is ready to review and send, and the draft can include attachments the product has created or edited. It can do this because it learns continuously from the entire inbox and from meetings, through a built-in local meeting notetaker that captures context in 99 languages without a bot joining the call. Answering a client, in other words, collapses from a multi-tool errand into a single click of review.

The pricing reflects the same focus on getting started: a free tier with no credit card required, Plus at $20 a month for higher AI limits and unlimited meeting recordings, and Max at $100 a month for intensive AI work. Testimonials on the company's own site tell the same story as its investors — one delivery lead says her entire team was using Scape a week after she first tried it.

Why investors bet on a Gmail challenger

Challenging Gmail is a graveyard of a category, and Scape's backers know it. The $3.2 million round that accompanied the July 2026 stealth exit came from Y Combinator, General Catalyst and FundersClub, with angels including Legora co-founder Max Junestrand, King co-founder Sebastian Knutsson, Ramp and EQT's Jacob Wallenberg Jr., former Spotify CMO Sophia Bendz, and operators from OpenAI, Google, Meta and Ramp.

The conviction quote came from Gustaf Alströmer, General Partner at Y Combinator: it is painfully obvious that AI will transform email, he said, and while he has seen hundreds of startups try to innovate in the space, he has seen nothing like the team behind Scape. Once people start using the product, they just don't go back to Gmail. Business Insider framed the same story as a new AI challenger in the quest to kill Gmail.

That retention claim is the whole investment case. Email is the rare category where the incumbent is both universal and unloved, which means a challenger does not need to convince people email matters — it needs to survive the first week of switching. Scape's answer is to make the first week feel like the work is already half done when you open your inbox. Hagberg built the company by following his own frustration to that answer; whether a five-person team from Stockholm can hold it against Google is the next chapter, and it is now playing out in public — Scape launched on Product Hunt in October 2026, with early access open at scape.app.

The playbook

  1. Build for your own frustration first — Hagberg's support tool became Scape only when he realized it solved his own inbox pain better than customers' — the pivot came from lived use, not market research.
  2. Prepare the work, don't chat about it — Scape's bet is that professionals don't need another chatbot or Gmail plugin; they need drafts, attachments and context assembled before they open a thread.
  3. Make the first week undeniable — YC partner Gustaf Alströmer's verdict — once people start using it, they just don't go back to Gmail — is the retention loop a challenger inbox has to earn.
