He Spotted a 10-Year Gap Between Markets. Then He Built the Platform to Close It.

Dmitry Yurchenko started in 2010 importing a product the CIS market had never seen, turned online courses into a $12M-a-year business, failed in the US, and pivoted into AXL — an all-in-one sales and marketing platform that now hands 1,200+ actions to your AI via MCP.

The gap is the opportunity

Born in the USSR, Dmitry Yurchenko studied in the US and China before his entrepreneurial career began — and the first move set the pattern for everything after. After an entrepreneurship course at MIT Sloan in Boston in 2010, he spotted a product the CIS countries simply did not have: PERS, personal emergency response systems. He brought the technology home, adjusted it to the market's needs and mentality, and built a business around it. The wins came fast — a Forbes 30 Under 30 listing, an Ernst & Young Entrepreneur of the Year finalist spot, and plenty of startup awards — even while revenues were still comparatively low. As he put it later, that was when PR helped the business a lot.

The pattern was not luck. Yurchenko's method, repeated across a decade of ventures from the Maldives to Indonesia to Latin America, is to look for opportunities that seem unobvious and offer time or money arbitrage: something proven in one market, absent in another, separated by a gap in years rather than in technology.

Bali, the Maldives, and a 10-year lag

After PERS came a glamping-tourism and event-entertainment venture on an inhabited island in the Maldives, and then Bali, Indonesia — one of the most successful development and edutainment projects there, which he eventually cashed out of, bringing a steady flow of CIS digital nomads with him on the strength of his personal brand in online education. His work there, he says, helped make Bali a popular destination for online entrepreneurs and freelancers.

Bali is also where the gap became measurable. Looking at local mentality, traditions, and what social-media marketing looked like there, Yurchenko estimated a 10-year lag behind Western countries. So he tried importing the online-education and marketing technologies that had already proved themselves elsewhere. Trial launches in Indonesia and other Southeast Asian countries taught him the second half of the lesson: audience readiness and perception lagged by about 10 years too. You cannot just import the technology. For online education, you have to overcome cultural obstacles first — but it is worth it, because being among the first pioneers buys ten years of benefit. An Uber or Amazon clone can be copied fast and easily. A market for online courses has to be created before it can be served.

The course business — and the failed American launch

The business Yurchenko is most passionate about is online education. In 2014, he and his wife launched their first online course and entered a market almost nobody around them knew existed — even though the same model was already worth millions in the US. They became pioneers in the CIS info-business, and by 2017 the course business was making more than $12 million a year, a level it has held since. In 2019 came the pivot: investing in IT development to turn the operation into a platform, AXL.tech, an all-in-one solution for online schools that lets professionals enter global markets and start courses with zero experience.

The road included a real failure. In 2017, Yurchenko tried launching a similar-to-CIS model in the US — publishing books and articles on a free-publicity model — and it simply did not work for Americans. His lesson is blunt: if he had not believed in success or tried over and over, he would not be an entrepreneur with a ten-year background. AXL.tech is different from that failed model, he argues — a platform that gives people the opportunity to grow and launch a course, with white-label courses such as “Expert kitchen” run to study the audience deeper.

Every market prices the gap differently

Pick a market, and Yurchenko will price the gap for you. India was estimated at $2.8 billion, with $10.4 billion projected by 2025; Southeast Asia looked more attractive still; Latin America is full of celebrities and bloggers who earn money on advertisements and do not make online courses at all. The differences are cultural as much as economic: in CIS, Latin and Asian countries there are “social lifts” and role models matter — a person becomes popular, earns on product placement and advertising, then starts selling their own products. In mature markets, creators monetize clubs and courses most of all. In Europe, it is still uncommon for a blogger to launch and monetize a course; people tend to dream of working for a Nokia or IBM instead of freelancing or building a personal brand. His advice to newcomers is a question: if not now, then when?

That cross-cultural lens is the through-line of his career: every venture was an arbitrage between what one market takes for granted and another has not yet learned to want.

Selling is still the hard part

The latest chapter brings the story full circle. Building a product got easier with AI; selling it is still hard — founders have to stitch together a website, email, CRM and more, like building a second product to sell the first. AXL's answer, launched on Product Hunt in November 2026, is a GTM copilot: an all-in-one sales and marketing platform — pages, quizzes, webinars, courses, video hosting, email, chat, CRM, telephony and AI calls — with a lead-scoring model trained on 1M deals and 300 parameters, and 1,200+ sales and marketing actions exposed to Claude, ChatGPT or Cursor via MCP.

The free plan — 500 contacts, 5,000 emails and 5,000 messages a month, no card, no trial clock — carries the same philosophy as the 2019 pivot: you do not pay until you find product-market fit. Yurchenko spent sixteen years spotting gaps between markets and building the bridge. With AXL, the bridge is the product.

The playbook

  1. Look for time or money arbitrage — Import what works in one market into a market 10 years behind — Bali's marketing lagged the West by a decade, and that lag was the opportunity.
  2. Become a beginner on every pivot — Every pivot starts from zero: “Your time is limited, so don't waste it living someone else's life.” Put yourself in a beginner's shoes and learn the market's mentality first.
  3. Create the market first — Uber or Amazon can be copied fast. Online education cannot — you have to overcome cultural obstacles first, but then you get ten years of pioneer benefit.
  4. Fail in public, keep going — The 2017 US launch failed and the company kept going. Belief plus repeated attempts is the job description, not a motivational poster.
  5. Let AI do the second product — Selling used to mean building a second product. AXL's MCP gives your AI 1,200+ go-to-market actions so founders don't have to.
