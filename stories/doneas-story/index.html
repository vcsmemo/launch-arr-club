How Bengeekly Turned His Own Accountability Problem Into Doneas

After selling Mailwarm to $1M ARR, Bengeekly kept hitting the same wall as a solo builder: no boss, no routine, no one to answer to. His answer was a 15-minute daily call with five other builders.

The solo builder's oldest problem

Being your own boss sounds like freedom until you realize it also means being your own accountability. Nobody notices when you sleep in. Nobody cares when the day dissolves into tabs and trivia. Bengeekly — a YC S20 founder who co-founded Mailwarm, the cold-email tool that grew to $1M ARR — knew the problem intimately. After the Mailwarm chapter, he kept running into the same wall as an indie builder: plenty of ideas, no structure to force them into the world.

In his launch comment on Product Hunt, Bengeekly tells the origin story without polish. Before Doneas, he tried the classic solo-founder hacks. KitKat bets — stake something small on shipping or lose it. Hiring an assistant to act as an accountability layer. The pattern was always the same: clever in theory, leaky in practice. A bet against yourself is easy to renegotiate; an assistant you pay for has no real leverage over you either. What actually worked, he found, was other people — specifically, a small circle of builders who expected him to show up.

From KitKat bets to a daily call

The insight behind Doneas is that accountability is a social technology, not a productivity technique. Bengeekly's experiments converged on one format that held up: a short, regular call with a handful of peers who are each trying to ship. So he productized the ritual. Doneas is a daily 15-minute accountability call with five other entrepreneurs, built around a weekly planning rhythm that keeps the daily standup about execution instead of decisions.

The weekly cadence is deliberate. Every Monday, Doneas builders plan their week. Then each morning they join their circle for fifteen minutes — long enough to say what shipped, what's next, and where they're stuck, short enough that it can't turn into a meeting. Between calls, everything you ship is tracked on your public progress page at doneas.com/@, including Bengeekly's own. Visibility is the mechanism: an empty public page is harder to ignore than an empty to-do list.

This is where Doneas separates itself from the productivity-app graveyard. It doesn't sell a new system of tags, projects, and dashboards. It sells a calendar obligation to five people whose opinion you care about. The tagline on the site is blunt: plan less, focus better, ship more. Less planning, because the Monday session handles it in one sitting. More shipping, because the call makes skipping the day's work socially expensive.

Accountability as a product philosophy

Bengeekly didn't just launch a product on October 20 — he spent the days before the launch talking about the philosophy behind it. In a pre-launch thread titled "Who keeps you accountable when you're your own boss?" and a launch-day essay on Product Hunt, "What's your #1 rule to stay productive?", he laid out the thinking that shaped Doneas: for founders without a boss, the bottleneck is rarely tactics and almost always accountability.

The forum posts read like a founder stress-testing his thesis in public. The rule he keeps coming back to is deceptively simple: make the promise to someone else, not to yourself. The pre-launch thread collected builders trading their own answers — co-working sessions, body-doubling, public build-in-public commitments — and Doneas is essentially the formalized, productized version of the most popular answers. A standing call. A visible record. A small group that notices.

There is a lineage here that makes the story credible. Bengeekly isn't theorizing about what motivates builders; he ran Mailwarm to $1M ARR and has shipped other products since. He has lived inside the indie-builder discipline problem for years, tried the self-improvement toolkit, and concluded that the missing ingredient was always a room with other people in it. Doneas is that room, rented by the month.

Who Doneas is for — and what it costs

Doneas is explicit about who it serves: solo builders and entrepreneurs, not teams. The entire design — the small circle size, the fifteen-minute length, the public progress page — assumes a person who owns their calendar and their outcomes, and who is failing on the second part for lack of the first part's opposite: structure. It pointedly markets itself as human, not AI — the site says "no AI, on purpose" — because the whole point is that other humans are the feature.

The product is also deliberately narrow in scope. Doneas doesn't try to manage your tasks, your docs, or your calendar — it leaves all of that to tools you already use and concentrates on the single failure mode its founder kept living through: the day with no witness. The pricing is straightforward: thirty days free, then $25 a month, cancel anytime. It's priced like a gym membership for shipping, which is the honest analogy. Nobody buys a gym membership because they lack exercise knowledge; they buy it because the building, the schedule, and the people in it make the behavior happen. Doneas is a bet that the same logic applies to solo builders — that a daily call with five peers is the closest thing to a boss you can rent for twenty-five dollars a month.

The playbook

  1. Make accountability a calendar event, not a promise — Doneas doesn't rely on willpower. The daily 15-minute call with five other builders is a fixed appointment — the social cost of not showing up does the work that motivation can't.
  2. Plan in public — Every builder gets a public progress page (doneas.com/@) where shipped work is tracked. Making the work visible to the group raises the cost of an idle week.
  3. Batch the planning — The weekly Monday planning session turns the whole week into a plan once, so the daily call stays about execution — not about deciding what to do.
  4. Borrow the discipline that worked before — Bengeekly had already found that a human accountability loop beat tools: before Doneas he experimented with KitKat bets and a hired assistant. Doneas turns that loop into a product.
