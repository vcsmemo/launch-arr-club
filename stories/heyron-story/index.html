He Gave an AI Agent $100 to Make Money. It Built a $62,900-a-Month Business — Then the Slide Started.

Robby Houston, a non-coder working in operations, handed an OpenClaw agent $100 and a single instruction: make money. A viral TikTok, a paid community and a $49 membership followed — $62,900 in April, then six straight months of decline to $17,300 MRR.

The $100 experiment

Robby Houston is not a coder. He works in operations at a full-time job, and in February 2026 he ran an experiment that most people would have kept to themselves: he gave an OpenClaw agent he calls Ron a $100 budget and a single instruction — make money. No product spec, no market, no plan. Just an agent, a hundred dollars, and a goal.

The agent's first idea was to sell SWOT analyses on Fiverr. It went nowhere. That should have been the end of it. Instead, Houston filmed the experiment and put it on TikTok — and the video passed a million views. People did not want a SWOT analysis. They wanted to watch an AI agent try to build a business.

That audience changed everything. The agent's next proposal was not a gig but a paid community, and Houston built it: a membership that gives a solo founder their own AI agent, running around the clock on its own cloud computer, plus a community and a 90-day plan for launching an app that makes money. The product is called Heyron, and the experiment became the company.

Thirteen days to $8,400 a month

Thirteen days after opening, Houston said the business was at $8,400 in monthly recurring revenue. He told the story on The Koerner Office podcast on March 20, 2026, in an episode about the experiment, and the numbers kept climbing from there. March closed at $50,000 in monthly revenue. April hit $62,900 — the best month Heyron has ever had.

The growth engine was not advertising. It was the loop Houston had stumbled into: run the experiment in public, let the audience watch the agent work, and sell the thing the audience was watching. The TikTok video brought about 15,000 followers. Podcast appearances carried the story further. The membership was $49 a month, and the pitch was simple — your one-person company, fully staffed.

What the $49 buys is worth spelling out, because it is the whole business. Each member gets a hosted AI agent — OpenClaw or Hermes — on its own dedicated cloud computer, with files, memory and connections to more than 1,000 apps including Gmail, Notion and GitHub. It answers on Telegram, WhatsApp, Discord or Slack. The membership includes $15 of managed AI usage a month, and Solo Software Studio, a community of solo builders with a 90-day plan to a first paying customer. The site counts more than 2,000 community members.

The peak, and the slide

Then the slide started. May came in at $53,000. June at $42,500. July at $33,100. August at $26,500. September at $21,700. October at $18,200 — six straight months down, each one lower than the last. October was 71% below the April peak.

On July 22, 2026, Houston posted on X that the agent had built an $84k-per-month business in 67 days. The monthly revenue figures tracked since tell a more modest story: the best month on record is April at $62,900, and no month reaches $84,000. The gap is probably the difference between a run rate and collected cash, but the post does not say so — and the gap is the point. A founder's milestone post and the money that actually arrived are two different numbers, and Heyron's story has both.

By November 4, 2026, the business stood at $16,400 in monthly recurring revenue from 570 subscriptions. By November 12 it was $17,300 from about 600 subscribers, with $17,000 in the last 30 days and more than $314,000 in all-time revenue — less than nine months after the February 12, 2026 founding date. The bleeding, at least for the moment, looks like it has stopped.

What the decline is really about

The obvious question is what went wrong after April, and the honest answer is that nobody has published one. The pattern, though, is familiar to anyone who has watched a hype-driven launch: a viral moment brings a flood of curious subscribers, and then the ones who came for the show leave when the show becomes a routine. A million-view TikTok is not a retention engine.

Houston seems to know it. On November 2, 2026, he started running the 90-day plan in public on YouTube — the same build-in-public instinct that started the company, applied to the harder problem of keeping customers rather than finding them. And the pressure is real: on November 3, 2026, YC-backed Agent37 launched always-on agent sandboxes from $4.76 a month, a reminder that hosted agents are getting cheap fast.

That is what makes Heyron's bundle matter. Raw hosting is a race to the bottom; a hosted agent plus AI usage plus a community with a concrete plan is a product with a reason to stay. The $49 price only works if the member feels the agent is doing a job and the community is moving them forward. If either half goes quiet, the cancellation is one click away — and six months of declining revenue suggest plenty of members made exactly that click.

Why it matters now

Heyron is still, by any normal measure, a real business: $17,300 a month from 600 paying subscribers, more than $314,000 collected in under nine months, built by one non-coder with a $100 experiment and a phone camera. The story is not a cautionary tale about failure. It is a precise picture of what a viral launch is actually worth.

The first lesson is that the experiment was the product before the product existed. Houston did not build Heyron and then look for an audience; the audience arrived to watch the experiment, and the product was built for the people who showed up. The second is that a bundle beats a server: hosting alone would have been copied and undercut within months, and the community and the plan are what still justify $49.

The third lesson is the one the numbers force on you. A peak is not a business. April's $62,900 was real money, but it was also the top of a curve that bent down for half a year. The founders who last are the ones who treat the viral month as the starting line, not the finish — and who, like Houston now, go back to building in public when the easy growth is gone.

The playbook

  1. Let the agent pick the business — Houston did not hand the agent a product spec. He gave it $100, a goal — make money — and let it propose the business. The first idea failed; the second one became the company.
  2. Turn the experiment into the marketing — The experiment was the marketing. A TikTok video about giving an AI agent $100 passed a million views and brought about 15,000 followers before there was a product worth buying.
  3. Sell the bundle, not the server — Heyron is not raw agent hosting. One $49 price buys the hosted agent, $15 of AI usage a month, and a community with a 90-day plan — the bundle is the moat, not the server.
  4. Post the numbers, keep them honest — Houston posted the journey in public — the wins and the numbers. The claims that hold up are the collected ones: a run-rate post is not the same as cash in the bank.
