How Codédex hit $85k MRR making coding feel like Pokémon

Sonny Li went from failing CS student to teaching at Columbia — then quit to build the learn-to-code platform he wished existed. Gamification did the rest.

The failing CS major who ended up teaching at Columbia

Sonny Li's Product Hunt bio reads like a punchline: "Failing CS major to teaching millions how to code." It's not branding — it's the actual arc. He studied computer science at Ohio State, struggled through it, then moved to New York for a PhD program he eventually dropped out of. Instead of leaving academia, he went sideways into it: teaching computer science at Columbia, NYU, and CUNY.

Teaching is where the insight formed. Year after year, Sonny watched the same pattern: students would start an online coding course full of energy and vanish within days. He later put a number on it in a Glitch interview — 94% of people who try to learn to code online give up within days. "That's not okay," he said, "especially when coding literacy is one of the most important skill sets for the digitally native generation."

Before Codédex, he spent five years at an edtech startup and seven total at the intersection of education and technology. He wasn't a founder with a hunch; he was a practitioner who had watched thousands of learners fail in slow motion and had opinions about exactly why.

A Pokédex for code

Codédex launched in 2022 from Brooklyn with a thesis you can say in one breath: make learning to code as fun and collaborative as playing a Pokémon game with friends. The name is a deliberate play on Pokédex — "we're a bunch of gamers, so this is a hat tip to a franchise that practically raised us," Sonny told Glitch.

The product is a journey through a fantasy world built on 8-bit NES aesthetics: learners travel through the lands of Python, HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, earning XP, unlocking challenges, keeping streaks alive, and collecting badges. It's Duolingo's retention playbook applied to programming, but with a crucial difference — the curriculum is project-based. Learners build real things, not just tap through quizzes.

That project-based core matters because it attacks the actual failure mode. Tutorial hell — watching videos without building — is what kills most self-taught programmers. Codédex's structure forces output: you can't advance without shipping small projects. The gamification keeps you coming back; the projects make the time count.

Community as a growth engine

Codédex didn't grow on ads. It grew on vibes — deliberately. Sonny's title is "Founder and Chief Vibes Officer," and the company operates like a community that happens to sell a learning platform. They run Twitch streams, monthly coding challenges with cash prizes, and publish winning community tutorials on the platform itself — turning learners into contributors.

The team reflects the audience: founding engineer Asiqur Rahman is a college dropout and former top-ranked Overwatch player; curriculum developer Ellie Popoca is a 2024 UIUC grad who has been creating Codédex courses for three years. They hosted a Gen Z Builders networking night with Shopify in NYC, drawing 150+ young builders. Every event, stream, and challenge is both marketing and product — the community generates content, and the content attracts the community.

The company raised roughly $750K to $1M in pre-seed funding from firms including Hustle Fund, Worklife Ventures, and Goodwater Capital. But the revenue engine — $85k MRR, founder-reported — comes from learners paying for a platform that treats their motivation as the product's core design constraint, not an afterthought.

The content strategy compounds in a way paid marketing can't. When Codédex publishes a community member's tutorial, that member shares it with their network — recruiting the next cohort of learners and contributors. The monthly challenges (with cash prizes) give the community a reason to return even between courses. And the Twitch streams turn the founder into a familiar face rather than a logo. Sonny's "Chief Vibes Officer" title reads as a joke, but it's actually the job description: in a market where the product is motivation, the founder's energy is a feature.

The curriculum keeps expanding with the market — a new GenAI course teaching how large language models work under the hood, authored by the founding engineer, signals that Codédex isn't frozen in 2022's web-dev curriculum. For a learning platform, stale content is a slow death; the community-contributed tutorial pipeline is the antidote.

Why it works

The learn-to-code market is brutally crowded — Codecademy, freeCodeCamp, Scrimba, and a hundred YouTube channels. Codédex's wedge was never "better curriculum." It was a sharper diagnosis of why people quit: boredom and loneliness. The 8-bit world fixes boredom. The community fixes loneliness. Everything else is execution.

There's a second, quieter insight: the audience is Gen Z, and Gen Z learns in public, in groups, with aesthetics. A platform that looks like a game they'd play anyway has a lower activation cost than one that looks like school. Sonny's seven years in edtech taught him that the best pedagogy in the world is worthless if the learner closes the tab on day three.

At $85k MRR, Codédex is proof that in education, retention is the product. The curriculum is table stakes. The reason people pay — and stay — is that it never feels like studying.

The playbook

  1. Diagnose the quit, not the competition — Sonny didn't try to out-teach Codecademy. He identified the exact failure mode — 94% quit in days — and designed everything around preventing it.
  2. Make retention the product — XP, streaks, badges, and unlocks aren't decoration; they're the core loop. In education, the curriculum is table stakes — keeping learners past day three is the moat.
  3. Turn learners into contributors — Monthly challenges with cash prizes, community-published tutorials, Twitch streams. The community generates content that attracts more community — a flywheel, not a funnel.
  4. Hire your audience — A dropout founding engineer, a 2024 grad writing curriculum, a founder who streams. The team is the demographic, which makes every product and marketing decision instinctively right.
  5. Aesthetics are positioning — The 8-bit NES look isn't nostalgia for its own sake — it signals 'this is a game, not school' to Gen Z before they read a single word.
