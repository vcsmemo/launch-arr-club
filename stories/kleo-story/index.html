How Kleo hit $1M after LinkedIn killed its extension

Kleo grew to 70,000 users as a free Chrome extension. Then LinkedIn told the founders to kill it — in a single day. They rebuilt from zero and still hit $1M.

By Jake Ward ↗ · 2026-10-14 · View on MilestoneWins →
70,000+users on the original free Chrome extension
500k+combined LinkedIn followers of the founders
$1Mtotal revenue in one year, founder-reported
$0paid ads spent on growth

The day LinkedIn said stop

On June 20, 2025, Jake Ward posted four words that ended a product with 70,000 users: "LinkedIn told us to." The Kleo Chrome extension — a free tool for LinkedIn content discovery that had grown entirely by word of mouth — was gone the same day. No ban, no published cease-and-desist. LinkedIn asked, the team complied, and the extension shipped its final update three days later.

For most founders, this is the obituary. Two years of work, 70,000 users, evaporated by a platform decision they couldn't appeal. But the Kleo story doesn't end there — because the founders understood something most extension developers don't: the users were never the asset. The audience was.

Jake Ward (200K LinkedIn followers) and co-founder Lara Acosta (350K followers) had built their following by doing exactly what Kleo helped others do — publishing consistently, studying what works, engaging daily. Combined with co-founder Cam Trew, the team commanded an audience of over 500,000 LinkedIn followers. LinkedIn could kill the extension. It couldn't kill the distribution.

70,000 users, zero ad spend

Kleo started as a side project Jake built to solve his own problem: collecting content ideas and writing consistently on LinkedIn. The Chrome extension helped users discover trending posts, analyze top creators' strategies, find proven formats, and format their own posts. It was free, genuinely useful, and spread through the exact community it served — LinkedIn creators telling other LinkedIn creators.

The growth loop was elegant: the tool helped you grow on LinkedIn, and growing on LinkedIn meant more people saw you using the tool. It hit 70,000 users with zero paid ads, and along the way helped Jake himself reach 100,000 followers in under ten months. The product was the marketing.

Jake's background shaped the product's DNA. He ran Content Growth, an SEO agency, and co-founded Byword, a platform for AI-generated articles at scale. In 2023 he wrote the post the industry called the "SEO Heist" — about redirecting 3.6 million organic visits from a competitor using AI — which journalists covered and Google's CEO Sundar Pichai commented on. Kleo applies the same philosophy to LinkedIn content: don't guess what works, study what's already winning and reverse-engineer it.

The co-founder story has its own drama. Lara Acosta grew up in the same hometown as Jake; when he called with the Kleo idea, she was traveling — and cancelled her flights, eating about $5,000 in bookings, to commit fully. "No grand plan. Just belief in the idea and the team," as the Kleo site tells it. That kind of commitment is hard to manufacture, and it shows in the product: Kleo combines Jake's distribution and SEO instincts with Lara's creator frameworks and Cam Trew's builder mentality (he went from 90-minute posts to sub-20-minute posts using the tool he helped build).

The rebuild

The rebuild is the most instructive part. Instead of fighting LinkedIn or trying to sneak back with a modified extension, the team rebuilt Kleo as a full web platform — V3 — that performs no actions inside your LinkedIn account. No automation, no scraping, nothing for LinkedIn to object to. It's now a complete AI personal-brand system: idea capture, writing in your own voice (trained on your style, not generic AI output), graphics, scheduling 30 days of posts, and analytics.

The positioning sharpened, too. Against ChatGPT and Claude, Kleo's argument is that general-purpose AI starts every conversation from scratch, while Kleo learns your expertise, audience, goals, and past content — "an AI content partner that's been working with you for months." The $99/mo price point reflects that it's selling to professionals whose LinkedIn presence drives real revenue, not hobbyists.

The numbers validate the rebuild: $1M in total revenue within a year, founder-reported. The extension died; the business didn't, because the business was never really the extension. It was the trust of 70,000 users and an audience of half a million.

V3's design reflects hard-won lessons about AI writing tools. The core complaint about AI content — that it all sounds the same — is addressed structurally: Kleo learns your tone, stories, and opinions rather than generating generic posts. It also owns the full workflow around writing, from capturing ideas to scheduling to analytics, because the team learned that the bottleneck was never just drafting — it was the friction of the entire publishing loop. At $99/mo with no trial, it's priced for professionals who can measure the return in inbound leads, not for dabblers.

Why it works

Kleo's story is a case study in platform risk done right. Every founder building on someone else's platform — Chrome extensions, social tools, API businesses — should study the sequence: comply immediately (don't fight the platform), rebuild on safe ground (web app, official APIs where possible), and lean on the one asset that's truly yours (your audience and reputation).

There's a second lesson about free products. The free extension wasn't a mistake that "should have been paid from day one" — it was the growth engine that built the user base, the brand, and the founders' own followings. Free bought them the audience that made the $99/mo rebuild viable on launch day. The extension was customer acquisition; V3 is monetization. Most founders get this backwards.

The playbook

  1. Free can be a strategy, not a mistake — The extension was free and genuinely useful for LinkedIn creators. 70,000 users came from the product being worth installing — the best growth hack is a product people recommend unprompted.
  2. Comply fast, rebuild faster — When LinkedIn said shut it down, they complied the same day — no legal fight, no drama. Then they rebuilt as a web app. Platform risk is real; the response is speed of rebuild, not prevention.
  3. Own your distribution — The founders' 500k+ combined LinkedIn following meant the relaunch had instant distribution. Your audience is the only asset a platform can't take away.
  4. Study winners instead of guessing — Jake's SEO background (the famous 'SEO Heist' post) shaped everything: Kleo helps users find proven content and reverse-engineer what works instead of creating from scratch.
