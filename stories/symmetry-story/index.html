How Symmetry hit $240k a month: a 22-year-old dropout's gym app conquered the most crowded category on the App Store

Mauro Botanes was told he was obese in high school. A year after launching Symmetry — a workout tracker for Spanish speakers — it passed 3 million downloads, topped Spain's Health & Fitness charts, and was pulling in around $240,000 a month.

The kid who was told he was obese

Mauro Botanes grew up in Spain, and in high school a doctor told him he was obese. That moment stuck with him — not as trauma, but as fuel. He threw himself into fitness, learned everything he could about training, and eventually realized the gym apps he was using were all built for someone else: English speakers, generic programs, no real guidance.

Workout trackers might be the most crowded corner of the App Store. There are thousands of them, and nearly all of them do the same thing: log your sets, chart your progress, remind you to show up. When Mauro — a college dropout — launched one more in June 2025, the sensible reaction was a shrug. He was 21 years old, with no funding, no team of growth hackers, and no distribution advantage whatsoever.

Twelve months later, that app, Symmetry, had passed 3 million downloads, ranked #1 in Health & Fitness on Spain's App Store, and was bringing in roughly $240,000 a month. He is 22 now. The story of how he got there doesn't follow the familiar arc of young founders who blow up consumer apps. It has a failed challenge, a very slow first year, and a strategic choice that most builders would dismiss too quickly.

An industrial-scale content machine

Symmetry's growth engine looks nothing like a typical app marketing plan. Mauro built what he describes as an industrial-scale user-generated content operation: nearly 80,000 videos posted across more than 500 accounts, generating a billion views in a year. This wasn't a few TikToks going viral by accident. It was a system — hundreds of creator accounts, all producing gym content in Spanish, all pointing back to the app.

The economic mechanism behind the machine is what makes it durable. Instead of paying creators flat fees that end when the campaign ends, Mauro built a profit-sharing model that gives creators nearly half of revenue. That aligns incentives for long-term content production: creators keep posting because they keep earning, and the content keeps compounding. It's the opposite of the one-shot influencer campaign that most fitness apps rely on.

The choice of Spanish was deliberate too. While every other gym app fought over the English-speaking market, Mauro built for Spanish speakers — a huge, underserved audience with strong fitness culture and almost no premium native-language options. Symmetry describes itself as designed by gym experts, positioning itself as a personal trainer replacement rather than just another tracker. That positioning, combined with native-language content, created a moat that bigger competitors couldn't easily copy.

The scale also created a defensive moat that's hard to appreciate until you try to replicate it. A competitor can copy the app's features in months, but they can't copy 80,000 videos, 500 established accounts, and a creator network earning real money. Content at that volume becomes infrastructure — the kind of asset that looks like marketing from the outside and functions like distribution from the inside.

Test everything: the experiment framework

If the content machine was Symmetry's growth engine, disciplined experimentation was its steering wheel. Speaking on Starter Story, Mauro described a product philosophy that evolved through three phases: building what the founders wanted (terrible), building what users requested (mediocre), and building what controlled experiments proved valuable. That last phase is where everything changed.

The team runs on quarterly OKRs with a relentless A/B testing habit. A single A/B-tested onboarding screen raised retention by 3% — a massive number at their scale. Meanwhile, a redesigned core workout screen moved nothing at all, which reinforced Mauro's rule that every change must be tested and intuition must be checked at the door. The team's default assumption is that they don't know what works until the data says so.

For a 22-year-old leading a team generating a billion views a year, the takeaway is deliberately unsexy. Mauro's advice to founders is to track everything, test everything, and play long-term games with long-term people. Systematic iteration — not intuition, not a viral moment, not a clever growth hack — is the actual moat. The billion views were the output; the experiment framework was the input that made them repeatable.

Why it works

Symmetry's success comes down to three compounding advantages. First, language-market focus: by building the best gym app for Spanish speakers instead of the fiftieth-best for English speakers, Mauro picked a fight he could win. Second, the creator profit-share model turned marketing spend into a self-reinforcing system — creators earn, so they post, so the app grows, so creators earn more. Third, the testing discipline meant every dollar of that growth was being optimized rather than wasted on untested bets.

It's also worth noting what Symmetry didn't do. It didn't raise venture capital to buy growth. It didn't chase the US market first. It didn't try to out-feature incumbents. Instead, it picked an underserved audience, built a content engine that scaled with revenue, and let experiments — not opinions — decide what shipped. In the most saturated category on the App Store, that turned out to be more than enough.

The playbook

  1. Pick a language market, not just a niche — Symmetry targeted Spanish speakers — a huge audience with almost no premium native-language gym apps — instead of fighting for English-speaking users.
  2. Build a content factory, not campaigns — 80,000 videos across 500+ accounts generated a billion views in a year. Systems compound; one-off viral hits don't.
  3. Share revenue with creators, not flat fees — Giving creators nearly half of revenue aligned incentives for long-term production instead of short-term promotion.
  4. Test everything, trust nothing — Three product phases — founder intuition, user requests, then experiments — with the last one winning. A single onboarding test lifted retention 3%.
  5. Play long-term games with long-term people — Mauro's stated philosophy: quarterly OKRs, profit-sharing with creators, and compounding iteration over growth hacks.
