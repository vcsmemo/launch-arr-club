How two physicists turned doomscrolling into $3.8K in 30-day revenue

Philip Sergelius and Max Hänze built NerdSip in the hours around their semiconductor day jobs — five-minute courses on anything, a blog that brings 40% of new users, and less than $500 ever spent on ads.

Half an hour a day, scrolled away

Philip Sergelius is 37, born in Finland, living in northern Germany since 1992. He and his co-founder Max Hänze have similar lives: two kids each, a background in academic physics, and full-time jobs in the semiconductor industry. Their side hustle gets built in whatever time is left over — early mornings, evenings after bedtime, the occasional quiet hour on a weekend.

The idea came from their own commutes. Both were spending half an hour a day scrolling feeds that left them with nothing, and they wanted that time back. NerdSip turns those minutes into general knowledge you actually keep: type in anything you are curious about — Roman aqueducts, how interest rates work, the biology of sleep — and the app generates a complete course with text, illustrations, quizzes and infographics. Lessons are short enough to finish on the train, and fun enough that it does not feel like studying.

They wrote the first line of code in December 2025 and launched on March 1, 2026, on iOS and Android. Not even five months later they were at 10,000 downloads, a 4.8-star rating across 150 reviews, and just past 100 paying subscribers — and crossing 100 felt bigger than any download milestone. A download is curiosity, Philip said. A subscription means someone decided a thing built at the kitchen table is worth their money every month.

Remove the lane, don't pick one

Everyone else picked a lane, Philip says. We used AI to remove the lane entirely. Instead of a fixed catalogue of subjects, NerdSip generates a course on whatever the learner types in, and every course flows into a shared feed — so whatever you just learned gets passed on to other curious people, and in return your feed fills up with the courses others found most fascinating. The best ones rise to the top on their own. Thousands of strangers are effectively building one giant pool of general knowledge together, and it grows every single day. Nobody curates it. It curates itself.

On top of that sits a game layer, with streaks, rankings, friends and item drops. Philip spent two weeks building a 3D character with items and sets to equip, as if it was Diablo. Nobody uses it, he admits, but it is cool — and every app needs one extremely overdesigned feature nobody uses. The part he gets genuinely excited about is the algorithm, which Max built: the ranking engine behind the feed.

Every course also passes through four layers of fact-checking before it reaches the app — written against live sources, scored nightly, gated on failure, and reviewable by a person at one tap. For a product that teaches general knowledge to strangers, that pipeline is the product as much as the courses are.

The blog is the growth engine

Almost everything has been organic so far. The founders spent less than $500 on ads in total, spread across small tests on Reddit, TikTok and the App Store — and none of them beat what they get for free. What works is the blog. SEO drives over 40% of new users, all flowing through nerdsip.com/blog, which has become the backbone of the whole operation. People land there searching for things like how to improve their general knowledge, and enough of them stick around to try the app. AI search is second at 28%, with ChatGPT and Google's AI answers citing the blog. Everything else is noise.

TikTok is the strange one. The videos have passed 10 million views across four accounts, but conversion is weak — roughly one new account per 10,000 views. They kept at it anyway, because the format works and the views are free, and lately the curve has started to bend: recently they had their first day with 60 new signups from TikTok alone. Panoramic slideshow posts regularly break 100,000 views. Along the way, two newspapers wrote about them — one of them a Sri Lankan newspaper they never reached out to. A third interview was already scheduled when Philip told the story.

The blog's Search Console numbers tell the same story: about 3 million impressions and 13,000 clicks, with a strong upward trajectory and a ton of articles on the first page of Google — even if the click rate is low, because the answers increasingly show up in AI citations instead.

The numbers, and the research bet

The revenue followed the same slow-then-fast curve. They generated $3,000 in July 2026, with a 30-day revenue chart showing $3,250 and a 350% increase on the previous period. By September the app had reached 10,000 registered users six months after launch, rated 4.7 out of 5 stars across the App Store and Google Play. By November 2026 NerdSip was doing $3,844 in 30-day revenue across 384 active subscriptions, with $16,733 in all-time revenue. Subscriptions cost $7.99 a month or $49.99 a year, under the name NerdSip Gold, with a 7-day free trial.

The next experiment is the one Philip is most excited about. They are sitting on more than 30,000 completed lessons, and they are analyzing that data to publish original research on attention spans and learning behaviour. A first study is already out: a preregistered analysis of 39,867 quiz outcomes from 2,590 learners, published as an open preprint in September 2026, with the dataset frozen in July and the code shared. If it lands, Philip says, that is publicity money cannot buy — and it is also the most honest marketing a learning app can do: testing, in public, whether its own courses actually teach.

The playbook

  1. Build for your own commute — The product exists because the founders were losing half an hour a day to feeds that left them with nothing. They built the fix for their own commute first.
  2. Remove the lane, don't pick one — Every other micro-learning app picked a subject lane. NerdSip lets AI generate a full course on anything, so the catalogue is whatever learners are curious about.
  3. Make the blog the engine — SEO brings over 40% of new users through the blog and AI search another 28%. TikTok passed 10 million views across four accounts. Total ad spend: under $500.
  4. Publish the research, not just the product — They preregistered a study on their own learning data — 39,867 quiz outcomes from 2,590 learners — and published it as an open preprint. If it lands, that is publicity money cannot buy.
  5. Charge for depth, not access — One free course a day, no account needed. The paid plan unlocks generating courses on your own topics, podcast mode and review rounds — depth, not access, is what costs money.
