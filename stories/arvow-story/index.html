How Arvow hit $70K MRR: the founder who cloned his way to the top

Vasco Monteiro didn't invent AI SEO writing. He watched Jasper and CopyAI prove the market, then built Arvow for a niche they ignored — and it now does $70,000 a month.

Steal like an artist, ship like a founder

Vasco Monteiro is refreshingly honest about where Arvow came from. Back in 2023, he started Arvow, an AI SEO writing app — and he will be the first to tell you it was not an original idea. Jasper was already there. CopyAI was already there. The market for AI writing tools was proven, crowded, and printing money.

His take, shared in a recent interview, was disarmingly simple: the incumbents had shown what worked, so he built his own version for his own audience. Instead of trying to out-innovate Jasper, Vasco crammed Arvow into the same space with a sharper focus — SEO writing specifically, rather than general-purpose AI copy. It was the same playbook he had run before, and he makes no apology for it.

Before Arvow, Vasco built Vettted, a marketplace he describes as Upwork niched down for SEO services. Same philosophy: find a proven model, carve out a wedge, execute relentlessly. Nothing new — and that was precisely the point. In a startup culture obsessed with originality, Vasco's career is a case study in the power of the unoriginal idea executed with unusual discipline.

There is a lesson here that most founder advice gets backwards. The question is not whether your idea is new. The question is whether the market is proven and whether you can serve a slice of it better than anyone else. Arvow's $70,000 in monthly revenue suggests the answer, for Vasco at least, was yes.

Why the clone strategy works in AI

The AI writing boom of 2023 created a peculiar dynamic. Jasper and CopyAI had spent millions educating the market that AI could write marketing copy. Every blog post, every Product Hunt launch, every Twitter thread about AI writing expanded the total addressable market — and most of that demand spilled over to whoever else was standing nearby with a credible alternative.

Arvow positioned itself to catch that spillover. By specializing in SEO writing rather than competing head-on as a general writing assistant, it offered a clear reason to switch: if your job is ranking content on Google, a tool built specifically for that job beats a generalist every time. The positioning did the selling.

This is the underappreciated math of fast-follower startups. The pioneers pay the education cost — the ads, the content, the category creation. The fast follower pays only the execution cost, and in software, execution cost is mostly focus. Vasco's bet was that focus on one use case, done well, would beat breadth every time.

The strategy also de-risks the hardest question in startups: does anyone want this? When Jasper is doing tens of millions in revenue, the answer is visibly yes. The remaining question is only whether you can take a piece of it — a much easier question to answer, and a much cheaper one to test. At $70,000 in monthly revenue, Vasco's answer is definitive.

From marketplace operator to SaaS founder

Vasco's path to Arvow ran through the SEO services world. Vettted, his earlier marketplace, connected businesses with SEO freelancers — a niche carved out of the giant Upwork model. Running a two-sided marketplace taught him the economics of the SEO industry from the inside: who pays, what they pay for, where the margins leak, and what buyers complain about.

That operator knowledge became Arvow's unfair advantage. Most AI writing founders in 2023 were technologists chasing the GPT wave, building impressive demos in search of a customer. Vasco was a domain insider who understood the buyer's workflow — the briefs, the keyword research, the publishing cadence, the revision cycles — because he had sold into that workflow for years.

The transition from services marketplace to SaaS product is a well-worn path, but it works for a reason. Services businesses generate cash and customer intimacy; products generate leverage. Vasco essentially productized what he had learned selling SEO services, turning years of marketplace conversations into a product spec that already had product-market fit baked in.

It is worth noting what he did not do: he did not raise venture capital to chase the biggest possible outcome, and he did not try to build the everything-app for AI writing. He built the thing his former customers needed, and charged them for it. Sometimes the most sophisticated strategy is the most obvious one.

The $70K/month machine

Arvow's revenue — $70,000 per month as reported in late 2025 — puts it in rare company among bootstrapped AI tools. The SEO writing niche turned out to be deeper than skeptics assumed: content teams, agencies, and affiliate publishers all need a constant stream of optimized articles, and they pay for tools that fit their workflow rather than fighting it.

What makes the number impressive is the competitive context. Arvow grew this revenue alongside Jasper, CopyAI, Writesonic, and dozens of other AI writers — not in a blue ocean, but in one of the most contested categories in SaaS. Winning a slice of a proven, growing market turned out to be easier, and more lucrative, than creating a new one from scratch.

The broader lesson extends beyond AI writing. Every platform shift — mobile, cloud, crypto, AI — creates a wave of pioneers who educate the market and a second wave of fast followers who capture the value. The pioneers get the magazine covers. The fast followers often get the better businesses, because they skip the expensive mistakes.

Vasco's story is a useful corrective to startup mythology. You don't need a novel idea. You need a proven market, a specific wedge, deep knowledge of the buyer, and the discipline to execute while others chase novelty. Arvow is proof that the second mouse sometimes gets the cheese — $70,000 worth, every single month.

The playbook

  1. Clone proven winners, niche down — Don't invent a category — find one that's already making money (Jasper, CopyAI) and build for a specific slice of it (SEO writing).
  2. Sell into a workflow you know — Vasco's SEO services background meant he understood the buyer's job before writing a line of product code.
  3. Let pioneers pay the education cost — Jasper spent millions teaching the market AI can write. Arvow captured the spillover demand at a fraction of the cost.
  4. Services first, product second — Running Vettted generated the customer intimacy that became Arvow's product spec — with fit baked in.
  5. Execute on focus, not features — One use case done well beat a generalist tool for buyers with a specific job to do.
