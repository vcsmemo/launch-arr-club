How Vincent Alonzi turned free spy data into $1M MRR

Vincent Alonzi came up through dropshipping, built a review tool, then started Trendtrack in late 2024. Two years later it makes $1 million a month, with ten people, no funding — and a paywall turned inside out.

From dropshipping to the data layer

Vincent Alonzi is a French entrepreneur based in Dubai who learned e-commerce from the inside. He came up through dropshipping, then built Digifeel, a review tool for online stores, in 2023. The pattern of his career is visible in that order: first sell things online, then sell the people selling things online a tool, then sell them the data about everyone else selling things online.

Trendtrack started in late 2024 and launched publicly in spring 2025. The company is bootstrapped and has never raised money; the team is about ten people, run out of Dubai under a company called HVSCALE SERVICES. Alonzi documents the growth in French on his YouTube channel and Instagram, and in English on X as @Bixentee_, where he has around 23,000 followers. In a 2025 episode of the French podcast Next Step, where he goes by his nickname Bixente, he walked through his early days in e-commerce before starting Digifeel.

The early numbers arrived fast. In March 2026 the French tech podcast opensource introduced him as the founder of an e-commerce SaaS that had passed €3 million in ARR in under a year. His own posts filled in the curve after that: $4 million ARR on May 27, $8 million on August 26, $900,000 MRR on October 20, and $1 million MRR on November 9. Eighteen months from public launch to a million a month, with the whole climb posted in public as it happened.

Give the data away, charge for the unlock

The October 20 post that announced $900,000 MRR opened with a provocation: “We hit $900K MRR by doing the opposite of every SaaS advice you’ve read.” The first of the five counter-intuitive hacks that followed was the business model itself: “Don’t chase paying users. Chase free users. Most SaaS hide everything behind a paywall, so nobody feels the value before paying. On Trendtrack, our data is free.”

That is the whole wedge. Anyone can browse the store database, the ad libraries and the brand profiles without paying. The paywall sits on exactly one action: unlocking a single brand — its shop analytics, ads, landing pages and emails — for 24 hours. A lookup is the unit of value, and the plans are priced by how many of them you get: Starter at $49 a month buys 5 lookups a week and 2 tracked brands, Pro at $89 buys 30 a week and 10 brands, Business at $159 buys unlimited lookups and 20 brands, with API access from Pro up. The free tier is not a trial. It is the demo, the distribution and the habit, all at once — and it is why 200,000-plus e-commerce players use the product while only a fraction pay.

The logic only works because the underlying data is genuinely hard to fake. Ad spend comes from Meta’s own transparency data, store traffic from SimilarWeb, and the whole corpus is refreshed every 24 hours — 2 to 3 million new ads indexed a day. A spy tool is only as good as its last refresh, so the refresh is the product, and the free tier proves it on every visit.

The founder is the distribution

Trendtrack has no venture money behind it, so it has no paid-acquisition budget to hide behind either. The distribution is Alonzi himself, posting constantly: milestone posts on X, a French YouTube channel, Instagram, and podcast appearances in French. The October video on his own channel is titled around Trendtrack doing more than $10 million a year. A November profile of the company credits influencer marketing, SEO and affiliate distribution as the major contributors, claiming more than €100,000 of MRR from SEO and about €3 million generated through affiliation.

That last number deserves a pause. Three million euros through affiliation is a large claim for a company doing a million a month, and it is the company’s own framing rather than an audited figure. What is verifiable is the shape of the strategy: the founder posts, does podcasts constantly, and people talk about the product on Reddit and LinkedIn, with no real link-building. The audience is built in the open, in the language of the buyers — French for the home market, English for the global one — and every milestone post is itself a piece of distribution. The November 9 post announcing $1 million MRR ends with “Now let’s scale to $10M MRR”, which is both a goal and an ad.

The product keeps pace with the posting. Landing Pages, a module for finding pages that already sell, shipped on November 5, 2026. The catalogue keeps widening — shops, products, mail, ads, advertisers, landing pages — because each new surface is another reason for a free user to come back tomorrow.

The numbers, and what they are not

The milestone itself is dated November 9, 2026: “$1 MILLION MRR with our SaaS @Trendtrack_io,” Alonzi posted, “Now let’s scale to $10M MRR.” A million a month is $12.6 million annualized — the figure ARR Club recorded the same day. Twenty days earlier, on October 20, the company had posted $900,000 MRR. The two posts together describe a business adding about $100,000 of monthly recurring revenue in three weeks, on top of a doubling from $4 million ARR in May to $8 million in August.

Read the figure for what it is. MRR is recurring subscription revenue, and Trendtrack’s own telling puts the goal at $1 million a month before the end of 2026 — a goal it hit in November, with weeks to spare. It is not profit: ten salaries in Dubai, server infrastructure, affiliate commissions and tax all come out of it, and none of those costs are public. It is not thirty-day cash either, since annual prepayments can make a month’s cash look bigger than the recurring line. And it is founder-reported, not audited: the posts are the evidence, and the posts are also the marketing.

What stays private is what a buyer of the business would ask first. Churn, acquisition cost, plan mix, gross margin after data costs, and how much of the million comes from the $49 tier versus the $159 tier are all unpublished. None of that dims the achievement: a dropshipper who built a review tool, then a spy tool, then a data business doing a million a month in under two years — by giving the data away and charging only for the moment you want to look at one rival, closely, right now.

The playbook

  1. Give the data away, charge for the unlock — Everything is browsable free; the paywall sits on one action — unlocking a single brand for 24 hours. The free tier is the demo, the distribution and the habit, all at once.
  2. The founder is the distribution — Every milestone is posted on X the day it happens, plus podcasts, a French YouTube channel and Instagram. The audience is built in the open, in the language of the buyers.
  3. Freshness is the moat — Two to three million new ads indexed every day, brand profiles refreshed every 24 hours. A spy tool is only as good as its last refresh, so the refresh is the product.
  4. Sell the rival, not the dashboard — Nobody wakes up wanting another dashboard. They wake up wanting to see what their competitor is running. Price the answer to that question, not the software around it.
