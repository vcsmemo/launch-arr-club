How Nick Saraev built a $1M ARR dialer about 90% with Claude Code

Nick Saraev built Clairvo about 90% with Claude Code, sold it to call-heavy sales teams at $250 a seat, and hit $1M in ARR — with a handful of mid-market contracts carrying most of the million.

Ninety percent Claude Code

Nick Saraev did not set out to prove that AI can write software. He set out to fix a problem he could sell: in call-heavy industries like HVAC, plumbing and roofing, most outbound calls never get picked up, and every missed pickup is a wasted rep-hour. Clairvo, his outbound sales dialer, dials up to three lines at once, puts the first live answer through to a rep with their script and live guidance on screen, and runs the follow-up from every call. The pitch is simple — more calls picked up, more conversations, more revenue — and Saraev built the product to deliver it with AI doing most of the coding.

“We just hit a million dollars in ARR with our SaaS product... I know the whole point of this video is that we built it with Claude Code. Um, I would say that’s like 90% true.” That is Saraev in the May 2026 video where he announced the milestone, and the 90% is doing a lot of work. Claude Code generated 200 to 300 ideas for raising pickup rates, built the predictive pacing algorithm that decides how aggressively to dial, and ran simulations on historical call data to test them. The remaining work — long-running refactors, and increasingly the day-to-day coding — went to Codex and DeepSeek, chosen as much for token costs as for capability. The lesson Saraev draws is not that one model is magic. It is that the codebase stays model-agnostic, so the team can switch models as prices change instead of marrying one provider.

The AI-built origin is also the marketing. A founder with about 500,000 YouTube subscribers and 700,000 Instagram followers, who teaches Claude Code and AI agents for a living, building a real SaaS 90% with Claude Code is a story that tells itself — and it is one Saraev tells constantly, on his channel, in his paid community Maker School, and through LeftClick, his AI automation agency.

The audience was the distribution

Clairvo barely needed a go-to-market motion, because Saraev brought the market with him. His first customers came through his warm network and YouTube: high-touch sales to mid-market and larger companies in call-heavy industries, rolled out to their existing calling teams. One early rollout ran at one of his agency’s own eight-figure clients — a customer he already had, for a product he had just built.

The most telling number is not in the revenue line. On X, Saraev says a single 15-minute YouTube video drove 75 leads for Clairvo. That is the distribution advantage of a founder who is also a creator: the launch video is the ad, the tutorial is the sales call, and the audience is the pipeline. It also explains how the company could reach $1M in ARR without the usual startup apparatus — no cold outbound engine of its own, no paid ads mentioned anywhere in the story, just a founder with a large audience selling a tool that solves a problem his audience’s businesses actually have.

There is a second audience, too. The May video that announced the $1M milestone doubles as a pitch for Saraev’s paid community — the same community where he teaches the Claude Code workflows Clairvo was built with. The product and the teaching feed each other: the community supplies the builders, the builders supply the testimonials, and the product supplies the proof that the workflows work.

A handful of customers carry the million

The $1M in ARR does not come from thousands of small subscriptions. It comes from a handful of mid-market contracts, and Saraev’s own deal maths makes the concentration plain: one roughly 100-seat deal at $250 per seat is about $25,000 in MRR on its own — about $300,000 a year, nearly a third of the total. A few customers carry most of the revenue.

That shape has consequences. A single churned contract moves the number by six figures, and a single new 100-seat deal moves it the same way in the other direction. It also explains the pricing story: Clairvo went from about $100 to $250 a month per seat, raising the price while customers kept paying. When your customers are mid-market sales teams whose reps’ time is the expensive input, $250 a seat is cheap against a pickup rate that actually moves — and the price was discovered by selling, not by guessing upfront.

The model is high-touch by necessity. Clairvo sells into teams with existing calling operations, not to solo founders trying a dialer for a weekend. The rollout is consultative — the team helps the customer’s reps work the new system — because the value is in the pickup rate, not in the seat count. It is the opposite of the self-serve AI tools that dominate the rest of this directory: fewer customers, larger contracts, and a founder who already knew the buyers.

The numbers, and what they are not

Read the figures carefully, because this is a story where the source matters as much as the number. The $1M ARR is self-reported by the founder in a May 2026 video, repeated on LinkedIn in June, and has not been independently verified. No newer revenue has been published since May, so the figure is six months old — the milestone, not a current run rate.

Two cautions travel with it. First, the video contains client-impact figures that contradict each other — per-year numbers presented as per-month — so they are not used here, and neither should they be quoted elsewhere. Second, an auto-dialer is a regulated product in many places, and the video does not cover calling and consent rules; anyone selling or buying one should check compliance before dialing. Neither caveat changes the core fact: a founder with an audience built a dialer with AI, sold it to the teams he already knew, and reached $1M in ARR on a handful of contracts. The question now is the same one Saraev’s pricing answers — whether the next 100-seat deal is as easy to win as the last one.

The playbook

  1. Pick an expensive, painful problem — Clairvo fixes a painful, expensive problem for call-heavy teams: calls that never get picked up. Saraev’s rule from the video is to pick the problem companies will pay real money to fix, not a lukewarm nice-to-have like a to-do app.
  2. Let subagents propose, then keep only what works — Claude Code spawned parallel subagents that each proposed many ways to move the pickup rate; Saraev threw out the junk by hand and built the few ideas that worked, including the predictive pacing algorithm.
  3. Raise the price while people keep paying — Clairvo went from about $100 to $250 a month per seat. Price is discovered by selling and raising it while customers keep paying, not by guessing upfront.
  4. Stay model-agnostic — The codebase was built with Claude Code, but Codex handles long refactors and DeepSeek took some of them too. Keeping the code model-agnostic lets the team switch models as token costs change.
