How AI Toolbox hit $20K months: the honest Product Hunt post-mortem

Adi Leviim won #1 Product of the Day with 410 upvotes — then published the analytics showing it barely moved the needle. The real story is the years of audience-building that made the win possible.

By Adi Leviim · 2026-10-14 · View on MilestoneWins →

40K+Users
#1Product Hunt

The trophy and the truth

In September 2026, AI Toolbox 3.0 finished #1 Product of the Day on Product Hunt with 410 upvotes, leading from the first hour to the last. Co-founder Adi Leviim was genuinely happy — the badge went on the homepage the same day. Then he did something almost no founder does: he opened Google Analytics and published every number, including the unflattering ones.

The #1 finish sent 539 sessions to the site across two days. AI Toolbox's seven-day average before launch was 1,930 sessions a day — so the entire Product Hunt effect amounted to roughly 28% of one ordinary day's traffic. Of those 539 visitors, only 23 ever reached a page with a price on it, even with a 50% launch discount live. Three reached the install-success page.

Leviim's honest read: launch-day traffic doesn't shop. People browsing Product Hunt are reading headlines, not evaluating purchases. The spike was real — launch day ran 37% above the seven-day average — but he calls it closer to a good newsletter send than a growth event.

Why publish numbers that make the win look smaller? Because, as he puts it, he had read a lot of launch retrospectives that end at the trophy, and never once found the number he actually wanted: what the trophy is worth. His post is the answer he wished existed.

The audience did the work, not the day

The most important sentence in Leviim's retrospective is this: we did not win the day because of what we did on the day. AI Toolbox had been in the Chrome Web Store for years under its old name, ChatGPT Toolbox, and had accumulated tens of thousands of users. Version 3.0 was a rebuilt, renamed, four-platform launch — and a large share of those existing users showed up to vote.

Beyond the existing base, the votes came from LinkedIn, X, a WhatsApp group, a subreddit, a Discord server, and friends asked directly. No paid promotion, no upvote exchange. Every launch checklist describes the asking; almost none describes the years of audience-building that make the asking work.

This reframes the entire Product Hunt game. The launch is not the strategy — it is the harvest. The strategy is the years of shipping, supporting users, and building in public that precede it. Founders who treat launch day as the starting gun have it backwards; it's the finish line of work nobody saw.

Leviim is blunt about the implication: if your runway depends on launch day converting, the plan has a problem no amount of upvotes fixes. Design the day so you win even if it converts nothing — and his team did, which brings us to what the day was actually worth.

What a #1 finish is actually worth

If the traffic was a quarter of a normal day, why bother launching at all? Leviim names three durable returns, none of them the spike. First, the badge is permanent — it sits in the homepage hero, a third-party credibility signal that every future visitor sees. For a Chrome extension asking for browser permissions, borrowed credibility is worth more than a traffic bump.

Second, eleven reviews at 4.9 stars — public, dated, attached to a high-authority page, from people with no reason to write them. That's a durable SEO and trust asset in a way session counts never are.

Third, the listing itself. A Product Hunt page ranks in Google, gets cited by AI assistants when people ask for tools in a category, and keeps working long after launch day. Leviim's team built their whole content strategy around being findable; the launch page is the same game played on someone else's domain.

The product behind the launch deserves mention too: AI Toolbox adds folders, full-text search, a prompt library, and conversation export inside ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok — used by 40,000+ people across 150+ countries. The extension solves the mess that power users of AI chatbots actually feel: hundreds of conversations with no organization. That real utility is why tens of thousands of users existed to vote in the first place.

Seven years of shipping, one honest post

Adi Leviim describes himself as having 7+ years of full-stack development experience, and it shows in how he writes about the business. His retrospective includes a section on what he calls the attribution trap: because votes happen on producthunt.com, your analytics only ever sees the return leg — the people who clicked through to your site. Any channel-by-channel ranking of what drove votes is, in his words, a feeling dressed as data.

He nearly published such a chart himself, then caught it: it would have been completely made up. That discipline — refusing to manufacture certainty where none exists — is rarer in startup writing than any growth tactic, and it's what makes the post genuinely useful instead of merely inspiring.

His advice for the next launcher is characteristically practical: point your launch link at the thing you want people to do, not your homepage (479 of his 539 visitors stopped at the homepage and never went further). Assume the launch converts nothing and design the day so you still win. Measure against your average, not your worst day. And be honest about where votes came from.

AI Toolbox does roughly $20,000 per 30 days in verified revenue. But the story that will outlast the revenue chart is the post itself — a founder who won the internet's favorite trophy and then told everyone exactly what it was worth. That honesty is a growth strategy too: it's why you're reading about AI Toolbox right now.

The playbook

  1. Build the audience before you need it — The #1 finish came from tens of thousands of existing users built over years — the launch was the harvest, not the strategy.
  2. Point launch traffic at one decision — 479 of 539 visitors stopped at the homepage. Send them to the pricing or install page instead.
  3. Design launch day for what survives it — Badge, reviews, and the listing outlast the spike. Judge the day on durable assets, not sessions.
  4. Never publish a feeling as a bar chart — Votes happen off-site, so channel attribution is guesswork. Leviim killed his own made-up chart before publishing.
  5. Honesty compounds — The candid post-mortem earned more lasting attention than the trophy — it's still driving discovery months later.
