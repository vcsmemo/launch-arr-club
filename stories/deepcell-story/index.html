How DeepCell Is Rebuilding Office Files So AI Can Actually Understand Them

Co-founder Amy Wang, a software engineer, built DeepCell after watching AI-generated office files that look right but understand nothing. The fix: a plain-text format that records the logic behind the document.

The files AI writes are the problem

Amy Wang is a software engineer, and she kept watching the same failure. Ask an AI agent to build a spreadsheet or a slide deck and you get something that looks finished — right colors, right fonts, plausible numbers. Then you dig in and find the numbers don't reconcile, the sources don't exist, and the reasoning that produced them evaporated the moment the file was written. The file was built for human eyes, and the AI that made it was forced to guess its way through a format designed for people, not models.

In her maker comment on Product Hunt, Wang explains what she and her co-founders built instead. DeepCell starts from a new plain-text file format — .deepcell — that records the logic behind a document, not just its layout. The spreadsheet, the report, the slides and the cheat sheet are all generated from the same underlying reasoning, so an AI can follow the chain of thought instead of reverse-engineering pixels. The product's tagline is "your deadline survival kit for office work," and the survival part is real: the nightmare it kills is the deck where a changed number doesn't flow through.

Four pillars: traceable, propagating, versioned, open

The founder's long-form Product Hunt post lays out DeepCell's design as four pillars, and they read like a manifesto against the way office software normally works. The first is traceable reasoning: every claim in the output carries its source, and cheat sheets ship alongside the deliverable so you can explain your thinking when someone challenges a number. This is aimed squarely at the deadline worker's real fear — not that the AI is wrong, but that you can't defend what it made.

The second pillar is automated propagation, the headline feature. One edit updates all your linked content: change a number once and the spreadsheet, the report, the slides and the cheat sheet all follow. Anyone who has maintained parallel versions of the same figures across three file types will recognize the pain this removes. The third is object-level versioning — track the evolution of individual objects inside a document rather than whole-file snapshots. And the fourth is openness: the .deepcell format is plain text, so it can be read, diffed, version-controlled, and audited by tools that were never designed around binary Office formats.

Together, the four pillars amount to a bet that office files are about to become machine-readable infrastructure rather than visual artifacts. DeepCell doesn't ask you to believe that — it just makes the file format the boring, reliable layer and lets the visible outputs be generated.

Who it's for — and the name coincidence

The target users are specific: people who live on deadlines. Analysts turning analysis into decks, consultants shipping reports, anyone whose week ends with "can you send me the spreadsheet, the writeup, and the slides?" The product positioning — "turns your work and ideas into ready-to-use docs, spreadsheets, and slides" — is about compressing the last mile of knowledge work, the part where your thinking has to become artifacts other people can consume.

Distribution is pragmatic rather than evangelistic. DeepCell ships as an AI agent plug-in, so it can be reached from the agents people already use, and it exports to Excel, Word and PowerPoint, so nothing in your existing workflow has to change on day one. It's a land-and-expand strategy for file formats: don't fight the incumbent file types, generate them from a better substrate and let the substrate win quietly over time.

It's worth noting the naming coincidence the team is navigating: there is an unrelated, well-funded Stanford spinout in cell biology also called DeepCell (deepcell.com). This DeepCell is deepcell.net, a young software company that launched on Product Hunt on October 20, 2026 — three days before this story was written. The products couldn't be more different; the search engines will sort it out eventually. It's an amusing collision, and a reminder of how crowded even good names get: the cell-biology DeepCell is a Stanford spinout that has raised roughly $100 million, while this DeepCell is three days old and giving its product away to individuals for free.

Wang's software-engineer background shows in the priorities: versioning, traceability, open formats — the unglamorous properties that determine whether AI-generated work can be trusted at scale.

The business model and the bigger bet

DeepCell is free for individuals, which matches the target user: the analyst on a deadline isn't waiting for a procurement process. A Teams plan for 3 to 50 seats is listed as coming soon, and enterprise is named on the pricing page. The launch-timing economics are familiar for AI productivity tools — get the individual users making things, and let the team motion follow the workflow.

The deeper story is the one Wang tells in her maker comment: that AI-generated work is only as trustworthy as the format it lives in. DeepCell's answer is to make the format itself the product — plain text, versioned, traceable, with every output a generated view of the same logic. If the bet is right, the next time your deadline slides update themselves when the numbers change, you won't notice. That's the point of infrastructure.

The playbook

  1. Record the logic, not just the layout — Instead of generating files that only look finished, DeepCell stores the reasoning in a plain-text .deepcell format — open, versionable, and readable by both humans and LLMs.
  2. Make every edit propagate — Change a number once and the spreadsheet, report, slides and cheat sheet all follow — because every artifact is generated from the same underlying logic.
  3. Meet users inside their existing workflow — By shipping as an AI agent plug-in plus Excel/Word/PowerPoint export, DeepCell fits into the office stack teams already use instead of asking them to migrate everything.
