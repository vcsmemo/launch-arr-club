How MonAi went from $300 MRR to $35K with one creator

Florian Vates spent eighteen months stuck at $300 MRR. Then one creator's videos did in a week what a year and a half of building alone could not — and he quit his job.

By Florian Vates ↗ · 2026-11-19 · View on MilestoneWins →
$35KMRR, April 2026
$300MRR after 18 months alone
10xMRR growth from one video
4.8★App Store rating

The $300-a-month years

Florian Vates is 31, married with two small children, and a full-time senior iOS developer and mobile UX lead at LOOP, a mobile agency in Germany. In 2023, when the AI hype was fresh and everything was getting more expensive, he wanted a project to play with the new tools — and a better way to track his own spending. So he built MonAi, an expense tracker that uses AI to make entering expenses easier: type it, speak it like a voice message, or automate it, and the app splits and categorises it for you.

He built the first version in a month or two, and launched it on Product Hunt on 20 June 2023, where it finished 25th of the day with only a handful of followers on X. He had dusted off his Twitter account that July and started building in public, posting lessons learned as he went — a mini-series on what he was learning while creating the app. The design was the point as much as the AI: a minimal, beautiful interface, no login required, everything stored securely in your personal iCloud.

For the first year and a half, almost nothing happened. He tried everything and barely made a few hundred dollars — about $300 in monthly recurring revenue. He had learned the hard way with an earlier app, CORDI, which he rebuilt for two years before launching at all; with MonAi he launched fast, but fast did not mean fast growth. The app was good. Nobody knew about it.

One creator, not twenty

At the end of 2024, the story changed — and it changed because of someone else. Charlie Alvarez, a Colombian content creator, found the founder's X link inside the app and reached out to offer a partnership. Alvarez's audience was in Colombia and Latin America, and his videos were in Spanish: story-driven, three a month on his main channel, each one showing the app solving a real spending problem.

The first video he posted multiplied MRR by ten within a week. Within a month it was at almost $8,000. Alvarez became Vates's business partner, and the two of them settled into a rhythm that Vates still describes as the whole strategy: work with one creator instead of twenty, and let that creator post three story-driven videos a month on his own channel. The audience fit did the work — Spanish-speaking viewers in Latin America who had never seen an expense tracker presented as a story rather than a feature list.

The lesson Vates draws from it is about distribution, not luck. He had spent eighteen months building in public, and the public part is what made the partnership possible: the link was in the app, the work was visible on X, and the creator could see exactly what he would be promoting before he ever wrote to the founder.

The dashboard and the leap

By April 2026, Vates was showing his dashboard on Starter Story. MonAi was at a bit over $35,000 in monthly recurring revenue — a subscription business on a 7-day trial with a hard paywall, run by a team of two plus an Android developer. The growth since the first video had been steady rather than spiky: from almost $8,000 within a month of the partnership to over $35,000 in about fifteen months, with the creator posting three videos a month throughout.

A month later he told Indie Hackers the fuller picture: his portfolio of apps was bringing in $40,000 to $60,000 a month, with about 90% of it from MonAi. And in January 2026 he had quit his job to work on his apps full-time — a decision the numbers had made for him, not a leap of faith. The portfolio includes other apps — Mindr, Re-Frame, Max — but MonAi is the engine, generating roughly 90% of his income.

The App Store charts tell the same story from the outside. MonAi holds a 4.8-star rating and has ranked among the top-grossing finance apps in Mexico, Portugal and Sweden — a long way from a 25th-place Product Hunt finish with a handful of followers.

What the app does

What the app does has barely changed since the first version, and that is the point. MonAi is an expense tracker that removes friction: enter expenses just like a voice message and the AI splits and categorises them automatically. No login, no account, no data leaving your iCloud. A free trial lets you try it first, and the subscription starts at $2.99 a month, or $24.99 a year.

The story is not really about AI, or about expense tracking. It is about what happens when a good product finally meets the right audience, and about how long that can take. Eighteen months at $300 a month is a long time to keep shipping, and most founders would have moved on. Vates kept building in public, kept the app small and focused, and kept the link where a creator could find it.

The through-line is the one he wrote himself in his build-in-public posts: launch soon, learn in public, and let one real partnership do more than twenty shallow ones. The $300 months were not wasted — they were the price of being findable when the right person finally looked.

The playbook

  1. Build in public, every day — He shared lessons learned on X from the start, and a creator found his link inside the app itself.
  2. One creator beats twenty — Three videos a month from one creator in Spanish beat twenty shallow partnerships. The audience fit was everything.
  3. Charge from the start — A 7-day trial and a hard paywall from the start. The paywall filtered for people who valued the tracker enough to pay.
  4. Quit when the numbers say so — He quit his job in January 2026 only after the portfolio was doing $40,000–$60,000 a month — the numbers made the decision, not the dream.
  5. Keep the team tiny — Two people plus an Android developer. No team, no office, no investors — the app stays small on purpose.
