One week, $700 MRR: how Andi turned iOS support chat into Reception

Users get stuck, never tell you, and just leave — so Andi and Frederick built the native in-app chat they wished existed. Seven days after launch: $700 MRR.

By Andi ↗ · 2026-10-26 · View on MilestoneWins →
$700MRR (Oct 2026, 1 week after launch)
7Days from launch to $700 MRR
Swift, React Native, ExpoPlatforms supported

The problem hiding in plain sight

Andi's pitch for Reception starts with an observation every app developer knows but few measure: users get stuck, never tell you, and just leave. Support emails get ignored. Reviews only come when someone is angry. The users who quietly churn never file a ticket — they simply disappear.

Andi and Frederick build iOS apps themselves, so they felt this one firsthand. Their answer was Reception: a native support chat that lives right inside the app, so the moment a user struggles, help is one tap away instead of an email form away. The chat is not a bolt-on webview — it is fully native, and it works with Swift, React Native and Expo apps alike.

The insight came from their own apps, not from market research. When you ship iOS apps yourself, you learn quickly that the loudest users are not the most important ones: the angry reviewer will at least tell you what broke, while the silent majority just deletes the app. Reception is built for that silent majority — catch them in the moment of confusion, inside the app, before the deletion. It is a small idea, but it reframes support entirely: not a helpdesk for complaints, but a safety net for revenue.

Support as a growth lever, not a cost center

Reception's positioning is what makes it interesting. Most support tools sell themselves as a way to handle complaints cheaper. Reception sells support as a revenue channel: send offers and paywalls right in the chat, ask happy users for App Store reviews at the perfect moment, and fix problems in real time before users churn.

The product also leans into the agentic era. AI answers from the developer's own docs first, and hands off to a human when unsure — so a solo iOS maker can offer responsive support without hiring a team. Replies come from a single inbox for the whole team, or straight from Telegram for founders who live on their phones.

Setup is deliberately frictionless for the vibe-coding generation: one prompt, pasted into a coding agent like Claude Code, Cursor or Codex, adds Reception to the app. That distribution choice matters — the tool meets developers inside the workflow they already use.

There is also a quieter, more human thread in Andi's launch post: 'it just feels good to talk to your users again.' For indie iOS makers, support is often the only direct line to the people using their work — and the incumbent tools were not built for them. Enterprise support suites like Helpshift or Intercom are powerful but heavyweight: priced and packaged for teams with support staff, not for a solo developer who wants to answer a question from Telegram between builds. Reception's wedge is being native, lightweight, and priced for the indie end of the market — the same playbook that has worked for countless developer tools that started by serving the smallest users first.

Seven days to $700 MRR

Reception launched on Product Hunt on October 19, 2026, with Andi introducing it in his own words: the thing that always bugged him and Frederick was users getting stuck and leaving silently. The launch post laid out the full thesis — make more money, get more reviews, lower churn — and invited makers to ask where a chat would help most: onboarding, the paywall, or when something breaks.

Exactly one week later, on October 26, Andi posted the scoreboard: Reception had crossed $700 MRR. The attached chart showed $717 in monthly recurring revenue, up $717 from the prior month — in other words, essentially all of it arrived in the first week. 'WHAAAT,' he wrote, and the reaction is understandable: most developer tools take months to find their first paying users.

What the first week proves is narrower — and more useful — than a growth miracle. It proves the pain is real and the wedge is sharp: iOS makers already know their users are churning silently, and a native chat that also sells and collects reviews is an easy yes. The next milestone to watch is whether week-one momentum compounds into a repeatable acquisition channel, or whether Reception's early adopters were mostly Andi's own audience. Either way, $700 MRR in seven days is a launch most founders would take.

The Product Hunt launch itself is part of the story. Andi did not launch with a generic announcement — he opened with the exact frustration, named the three outcomes, and ended with a question that invited every iOS maker reading to picture the feature in their own app. Early users, he reported, already saw less churn, more upgrades and more reviews. That kind of launch post does double duty: it explains the product and recruits its first believers in the same breath.

For MilestoneWins readers, Reception is also a useful case study in launch velocity. One week from Product Hunt to $700 MRR does not happen because of a single viral post — it happens when the product, the pitch, and the audience are already aligned before launch day. Andi spent the launch post speaking directly to iOS makers' lived frustration, and the milestone post a week later closed the loop with a number. That two-post arc — pain, then proof — is a template any founder can reuse.

The playbook

  1. Sell the outcome, not the feature — Reception doesn't lead with 'chat SDK' — it leads with more money, more reviews, less churn. Support reframed as a growth lever is a much easier purchase than support framed as a cost.
  2. Meet developers in their workflow — One-prompt setup via Claude Code, Cursor or Codex removes the integration tax entirely. When your buyers build with AI agents, your onboarding should speak agent.
  3. Post the scoreboard fast — Andi announced $700 MRR seven days after launch, with the chart attached. Early, specific receipts build the public narrative while the product is still new.
