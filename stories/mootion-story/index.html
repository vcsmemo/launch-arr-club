How Mootion turned lifetime deals into $100K+ MRR

Chao Tong asked one question before his first AppSumo campaign: how long does a Sumo-ling stay? The answer — 15 to 18 months — turned lifetime deals from a risk into the business model.

The question no tool answered

When Chao Tong and his team of AI scientists and engineers built Mootion in late 2023, they wanted to answer a question no tool had answered yet: how can someone with no filmmaking experience tell the story in their head? The video tools of the time made clips — ten seconds here, a slideshow there — but nobody could take a prompt, a script, an image or an audio file and return a complete video story.

By mid-2024, they had something. Mootion was one of the first tools to let users create full-length AI videos from text, images or audio — start-to-finish stories with scenes, characters, voiceovers and music, not just clips. Fifty thousand users signed up in the first month. The product was built for people full of ideas but short on time: creators, educators, marketers and filmmakers who wanted polished video without editing skills.

The company behind it is based in Hong Kong, and the team stayed small and technical — AI scientists and engineers building the models and the product together, rather than a sales organisation wrapped around a demo.

The lifetime-deal math

Chao had one question before he said yes to his first AppSumo campaign: what is the typical lifetime for a Sumo-ling? The answer was the number that convinced him — 15 to 18 months. With monthly credit caps built into the lifetime tiers, he could model exactly what a lifetime customer would cost, and the maths worked: the lifetime value of AppSumo users matched what the AppSumo team had seen across the platform.

Mootion launched on AppSumo in October 2025 with a few thousand monthly subscribers. It ran three campaigns in total — the first to test the model, the second as a data exercise, watching the AppSumo cohort and the subscriber cohort in parallel for any meaningful divergence. The credit caps were the control that made the whole thing work: lifetime buyers got a generous but bounded allowance each month, so heavy users could not eat the margin.

The result was the opposite of the usual lifetime-deal story. Instead of a one-time cash spike followed by a support burden, the campaigns turned price-sensitive global buyers into a growth channel: people who would never have paid $15 a month tried the product, stayed, and told others. The lifetime deal was not a discount — it was a distribution deal with the maths done in advance.

The product

What Mootion sells is the finished video. Turn a prompt, a script, an image, an audio file or a product description into a complete video story, with AI script and image editing, a character library, voiceovers in 17 languages, and watermark-free 1080p exports. Sixteen smart templates cover the common jobs — faceless short videos, bedtime stories, bilingual dialogue, educational explainers — and the available models include Seedance 2.5 and 2.0, Seedream 5.0 and MiniMax H3.

Pricing is deliberately simple and scales by credits rather than by seats. Standard is $15 a month for 1,500 credits, Plus and Pro step up to 8,000 credits at $50 a month, and Max is $200 a month for 32,000 credits, with an EDU plan at $6 a month. Creating and downloading videos both require a paid plan — there is no free plan for the full product experience — and the terms are blunt about it: purchases are final sale, reviewed case by case.

The product keeps moving. Mootion 5.0 shipped with short-drama series generation, turning a premise into connected dramatic scenes with characters, mood and pacing, and the model list keeps growing as new generation models appear. The team ships on top of the same core job it started with: take an idea in someone's head and return a watchable video.

The numbers

By June 2026, when AppSumo told the story, Mootion was at $100,000 to $150,000 in monthly recurring revenue — and the product was profitable. The AppSumo campaigns had done what Chao's maths said they would: prove that lifetime deals can work for AI products when the costs are modelled, the LTV is tracked and the usage is capped.

The traffic tells the same story from the outside. Reviewers tracking the product put it at roughly 729,000 monthly visits, and the AppSumo listing holds a 3.8-star rating across more than 150 reviews — the honest kind of rating for a product that sometimes renders a scene wrong and has to be re-run, and that its founder's team answers in public.

The lesson is narrower than it looks. Lifetime deals are not a growth hack; they are a pricing decision that only works when you know your numbers. Chao knew his: 15 to 18 months of lifetime, a bounded cost per user, and a product that turns a first-time buyer into a returning creator. Do the maths first, cap the downside, and the deal becomes a channel instead of a gamble.

The playbook

  1. Answer a question no tool answered — How can someone with no filmmaking experience tell the story in their head? The whole product is the answer to that question.
  2. Do the LTV math before the deal — Before the first campaign, Chao asked how long a Sumo-ling typically stays. At 15–18 months, the lifetime deal maths worked.
  3. Cap costs per user, not ambition — Monthly credit caps on the lifetime tiers kept the deal profitable while the user base compounded.
  4. Price by credits, not seats — Plans scale by credits — $15 to $200 a month — so the price tracks the value a customer actually uses.
  5. Keep shipping on the same job — New models, new tools, new templates: Seedance, Seedream, MiniMax. The product keeps shipping on top of the same core job.
