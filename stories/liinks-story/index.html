How Liinks hit $31K MRR: the link-in-bio underdog that out-designed Linktree

Charlie Clark built Liinks in one of the most crowded markets on the internet — and won by being cheaper, better-designed, and relentlessly personal. Solo, bootstrapped, no staff: from a January 2020 soft launch to $30K+ MRR.

Why build another link-in-bio tool?

Charlie Clark, a Brooklyn-based software engineer and creative coder with 12+ years of professional experience, spent six months building VSUAL — a print-on-demand marketplace for artists. While doing outreach to artists in 2019, he kept noticing the same thing: nearly all of them used link-in-bio tools on Instagram. And the tools were... fine. Not great. He saw a gap for something more powerful, better designed, and more affordable than the competition.

The market logic was simple. Almost everyone needs a link-in-bio page, and the core functionality is "essentially a stack of buttons linking out to various URLs." A huge addressable market plus a simple product equals an opening — if you can differentiate. On his Product Hunt launch post in February 2020, Clark framed the dual itch precisely: existing tools did either nice landing pages or Instagram auto-posting, "but nothing that does both in a simple, clean and affordable way." He reused much of VSUAL's infrastructure, code, and UX to build Liinks fast, soft-launched at the end of January 2020, and hit Product Hunt on February 26th — around 300 upvotes, #8 on the homepage.

Winning a crowded market the unscalable way

Clark's growth strategy reads like a manual for competing with giants when you have no money. First: cold outreach with free work. He recreated competitors' users' pages inside Liinks with better styling and asked them to switch — and built custom pages for people using no tool at all. Second: his VSUAL artist contacts became Liinks's first customers, onboarded white-glove, one by one. Third: relationships. "As an indie hacker, the best way to stand out from the big players is by building relationships with your customers," he wrote. "That's something the big players can't replicate!"

The product differentiation was real, not marketing. Instagram auto-posting — mention a URL in an IG post caption and it automatically appears on your Liinks page — was described by one reviewer as a feature they'd "never seen any other link in bio tool offer." Deep customization, a clean mobile-first editor, one-click Linktree import (paste your Linktree URL and Liinks brings over your links, socials, photo, and bio), plus advanced Pro features like custom domains, analytics, and multi-profile management for agencies.

And the pricing was a weapon: Premium launched at $3 a month — deliberately cheaper than Linktree. "Charging less can also be a competitive advantage," Clark noted. The milestones came steadily: first "internet dollar" within two months of launch, $1,000 MRR four months in (features plus a 14-day trial plus Instagram ads), $2,000 MRR two months after a price raise to $3.99, $15K MRR by December 2022, $20K MRR with 4,200 paying customers by July 2023.

Killing the free tier — and quitting smart, not fast

Two of Clark's decisions look counterintuitive and both paid off. First, he killed the free tier after phishing and spam abuse made it untenable. The result: fewer users, but higher-quality paying ones and better profitability. The pricing FAQ now frames it as a feature: "you're the customer, not the product." Second, after an early attempt at quitting his job to build full-time — a year on two products that didn't take off — he went back to the same job and built Liinks on nights and weekends for roughly four years, until it hit $27K MRR. "Quit smart, not fast," as the AppSumo deep-dive put it.

The paid-acquisition story is equally disciplined. Early Instagram ads worked until iOS 14 killed the targeting; then Google Search ads for "link-in-bio" terms took over from $1–5K MRR — "high intent, high ROI." Organic growth came from "Made with Liinks" badges on user pages and a free AI converter that turns any website into a link-in-bio page. And the multi-profile management feature — built in a single day — now drives roughly 25% of revenue.

By October 2024, the numbers were: $25K MRR, 5,300+ paying customers (about 4,800 on Premium at $4 and 500 on Pro at $10), 11% year-over-year growth, 80–90% margins, roughly $250K in the last twelve months. By September 2025: $27K MRR. Today his personal site reads: "founder of Liinks, a link-in-bio tool for creators, which he bootstrapped solo to $30k+ MRR." Still one person. Still no staff. Still no funding — the whole thing started on about $50 a month in server costs and a $500 Stripe Atlas LLC registration.

The boring consistency playbook

Clark is honest about the role of luck: "Luck plays a much bigger role in success than most people admit. Being in the right place at the right time can make a huge difference, so stay flexible, but also know when it's time to focus on scaling." But the through-line of the Liinks story isn't luck — it's boring consistency. Ship early, iterate on real feedback ("done is better than perfect"), keep the day job until the numbers say quit, and compete on craft and care where giants compete on scale.

Oh, and he has a sense of humor about the whole thing: on April 1st, 2026, he launched Stiinks.co — "the worst link-in-bio platform on the internet. On purpose." When you've bootstrapped to $30K MRR solo, you've earned the joke.

The playbook

  1. Compete on craft in crowded markets — Better design, deeper customization, and a lower price beat incumbents when the product is fundamentally simple.
  2. Do unscalable outreach first — Recreating strangers' pages by hand and white-glove onboarding bought the first customers no ad budget could.
  3. Relationships are the moat — "The best way to stand out from the big players is by building relationships with your customers. That's something the big players can't replicate!"
  4. Quit smart, not fast — Four years of nights and weekends to $27K MRR beat a premature leap. The day job funded the runway.
  5. Kill what's unprofitable — Dropping the abused free tier traded vanity user counts for paying customers and 80–90% margins.
