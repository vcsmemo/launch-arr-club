The Niche-Site SEO Who Turned “Why Do Weaker Sites Rank?” Into a Tool AIOSEO Wanted to Buy

Paul Pilavachi spent years building niche sites and watching weaker pages outrank stronger ones. That persistent question became LowFruits, a weak-spot keyword research tool that grew to a reported €20,000 in monthly recurring revenue — and was acquired by All in One SEO in May 2024.

A veteran niche-site builder with one persistent question

Paul Pilavachi was not an outsider who stumbled into SEO. He was a veteran SEO practitioner who had spent years building niche sites — the kind of operator who lives inside algorithm updates rather than reading about them afterward. And one observation kept nagging him: weaker sites were sometimes ranking for highly competitive keywords, and nobody could give him a satisfying explanation for why.

That frustration, reported in a SaaS Founders Club case study published in June 2026, became the origin story of the company. The case study — a second-hand retelling posted by Mher Hovakimyan, not an account in Pilavachi’s own words — frames the business as a direct answer to that single question: instead of complaining about the algorithm, build a tool that exposes the gaps it leaves open.

There is a particular honesty to this kind of origin. The frustration with algorithm updates was not theoretical for Pilavachi; it was the daily tax on the niche sites he operated. The case study presents a practitioner who stopped waiting for the industry’s conventional wisdom to explain an anomaly he kept seeing in his own data — and decided the anomaly was the opportunity.

October 2020: the question becomes code

According to the same case study, Pilavachi started coding the tool himself in October 2020. That detail matters more than it looks. LowFruits was not conceived in a pitch deck or a venture studio; it was built by a practitioner scratching his own itch, with the domain knowledge of someone who had spent years on the receiving end of keyword research workflows.

The product that emerged is best described as weak-spot keyword research: rather than competing head-on for the hardest keywords, the tool helps users find the low-hanging fruit — the queries where the current top results are weak enough to displace. The name is the thesis. It is a tool built from the exact frustration that created it, and its audience is people who share that frustration: niche-site builders and independent SEOs looking for winnable fights.

Starting to code in October 2020 also dates the product’s worldview. This was a tool conceived in the thick of the niche-site era, when independent operators were constantly recalibrating around updates — exactly the conditions that produced the original question. The timing was not a master plan; it was a practitioner finally acting on years of accumulated irritation.

A solo-built tool with a reported €20,000 MRR

The case study reports that by 2023, roughly three years after Pilavachi began coding, LowFruits was generating around €20,000 in monthly recurring revenue. That figure should be read with the caveat attached: it comes from a second-hand retelling, not from the founder’s own disclosure, so treat it as attributed rather than confirmed.

Even with that caveat, the shape of the trajectory is instructive. This was not a blitz-scaled, venture-backed rocket; it was a bootstrapped-looking, pay-as-you-go product — credits start at $25 — serving a narrow, well-defined user base of SEO practitioners. For an independently built keyword research tool, a reported €20K MRR three years in is the kind of quiet, durable revenue that larger companies start to notice.

The pricing model itself tells the story of the audience. Pay-as-you-go credits from $25 are priced for the way niche-site builders and freelance SEOs actually work — bursts of research around a new site or campaign, not a standing monthly subscription. The monetization fits the workflow, and the workflow is the one Pilavachi lived himself.

May 2024: acquired by All in One SEO

Larger companies did notice. In May 2024, All in One SEO — part of the Awesome Motive portfolio led by CEO Syed Balkhi — announced the acquisition of LowFruits. The announcement, written by AIOSEO president Ben Rojas, made a point of saying that “LowFruits will continue to operate independently, maintaining its unique features,” a signal that the acquirer valued the product as it was rather than as raw material to be absorbed.

Balkhi’s own quote in the announcement was brief and warm: “We are thrilled to welcome LowFruits to the AIOSEO family.” For a tool that began as one niche-site builder’s attempt to answer why weaker sites rank, joining one of the biggest names in WordPress SEO is about as clean a validation as a bootstrapped-style product can get. The practitioner’s side project had become a portfolio asset.

The independence clause deserves emphasis. Acquirers do not promise independent operation for products they intend to dismantle; the commitment to keep LowFruits’ unique features intact reads as recognition that the product’s distinct identity — weak-spot research, practitioner-first design — was part of what was being bought. Six years after the question, and four after the first line of code, the answer had a corporate home.

A 12-month case study, in public

LowFruits also practices what it preaches in the open. The company's blog ran a 12-month public case study of a BBQ niche site built using the tool's weak-spot methodology: 131 articles published, 78 ranking positions 1–3, 20,333 page views per month, and an average of $353.30 per month in revenue. The case study is authored by "Dim" — the company blog does not establish whether Dim is Pilavachi or a team member, so read the byline with that caveat.

The numbers are modest by design, and that is the point. LowFruits was never pitched as a lottery ticket; it was pitched as a repeatable method for finding searches a small site can actually win, documented month by month. For a tool whose whole premise is that weaker sites can rank, showing a real site doing exactly that — with the traffic curve attached — is the most honest marketing available.

What the LowFruits story teaches

The through-line of the LowFruits story is specificity. Pilavachi did not set out to build “SEO software”; he set out to answer one question he personally could not let go of, and the product is that question operationalized. Every strong detail in the story — the October 2020 start date, the pay-as-you-go pricing, the independent operation post-acquisition — flows from a founder who knew exactly who the tool was for, because he was the tool’s first user.

It is also a reminder that in SEO tooling, depth beats breadth. A narrow instrument that does one job — finding weak spots — and does it well can reach a scale that attracts acquirers with portfolios full of generalist tools. AIOSEO did not buy a feature; according to its own announcement, it bought a product worth keeping independent. That is the strongest possible endorsement of building something with a point of view.

And the arc has a satisfying symmetry. The veteran niche-site builder frustrated by algorithm updates ended up selling to one of the largest WordPress SEO companies in the world — not by abandoning the niche perspective, but by productizing it. The question that started it all — why do weaker sites rank? — turned out to be worth more than an answer. It was worth a company.

The playbook

  1. Start from your own unsolved frustration — Pilavachi’s product thesis was literally his own open question — why do weaker sites rank for competitive keywords? Build the tool you wish existed for your own work.
  2. Name the product after the insight — LowFruits’ weak-spot keyword research is the business in three words: find the easy wins the algorithm leaves exposed. The positioning does the selling.
  3. Grow on practitioner revenue, not venture timelines — From October 2020 to a reported €20K MRR by 2023, the arc is slow, durable, practitioner-funded growth — the kind acquirers read as real demand.
  4. Negotiate independence into the exit — AIOSEO publicly committed that LowFruits would “continue to operate independently, maintaining its unique features” — proof the product’s distinct identity was part of the deal value.
  5. Price for the way researchers actually buy — Pay-as-you-go credits from $25 match how niche-site builders and freelance SEOs work: sporadic, project-based research, not an always-on subscription.
