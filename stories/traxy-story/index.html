How an agency’s internal tool became a $54.6K MRR buyer finder

Ben Buaron ran a LinkedIn agency and kept watching the same pattern: clients who moved within moments of a like or comment closed deals, and clients who waited got nothing. He built a system to catch that moment — then turned it into traxy.ai, now at $54.6K MRR.

The timing problem

Ben Buaron runs Windmill Growth, a done-for-you LinkedIn ghostwriting agency for B2B founders. The agency had worked with more than 185 founders, kept about 30 on the books at a time, and by November 2026 was doing about $120,000 a month in recurring revenue of its own. And three months before he wrote about it, Buaron almost shut the agency down. Good clients were leaving, and he could not figure out why.

So he did the unglamorous work: days inside CRM notes, call recordings and engagement logs. “I wasn’t looking for a story. I was looking for the thing I was missing. And I found it.” Some clients were closing deals consistently from LinkedIn. Others posted great content every week and got nothing back. Same effort, different results. He thought it was a content problem. It wasn’t. It was a timing problem.

The clients winning were the ones who moved the moment someone liked or commented — DM, follow-up, meeting, deal. Fast, clean, every time. The clients losing waited for someone to check manually, waited for inbound that never came. “LinkedIn ROI isn’t made at the top. It’s made in the middle. Who engaged. How fast you moved. Whether that moment became a conversation.” That paragraph is the entire product, written before the product existed.

From internal tool to product

The next step was not a launch. It was a system, built internally: the moment someone engages with a client’s content — a like, a comment, a share — identify whether they match the ideal customer profile, instantly. It worked for Windmill first, then for every client they gave it to. “A few months later, we turned that system into a product. Because this wasn’t a Windmill problem.” Every founder he spoke to had the same pattern: warm leads sitting in their comments, nobody moving on them, intent dying quietly every single day.

That product is traxy. It scans LinkedIn likes, comments and posts across a target market every hour, lists the people who match the customer’s ideal profile by 50% or more as leads, each with the like, comment, post or profile view behind it, and runs automated or semi-automated outreach by LinkedIn and email. It can also be called from AI assistants such as Claude and ChatGPT through MCP. The official site credits three people with building it: Buaron, Adam Salmanov, who leads go-to-market, and Daniel Walker, the founding engineer.

Buaron built it in public. On April 15, 2026, day 32 of his build-in-public series, he posted 100 active users and 13 paying users, with about three new users a day on average. No revenue figure in that post — just the counts, posted while the product was still becoming one. The company behind it, traxy, inc., is based in New York, and the business was founded in January 2026, bootstrapped, with a team of two to five.

One plan, one job

traxy sells one thing, one way: a single Pro plan at $149 a month, with Enterprise on request, plus a done-for-you outreach service for teams that want the work done for them. There is no tier maze and no feature table to argue about. The job is find buyers already engaging in your market; the price is $149; the channel is the founder’s own audience, built through the agency the tool came from.

That is the agency-to-tool path, and it is repeatable precisely because it is narrow. Windmill already served the exact buyers traxy needed — B2B founders and sales teams living on LinkedIn — so the product launched into an audience that had already watched the problem being solved by hand. The AI Receipts’ verdict on the model is a plain yes: a narrow lead-gen product sold to an audience the founder already serves through his agency.

The distribution, like the product, is the timing insight applied to itself. traxy does not chase cold attention. It watches for engagement and moves while it is warm — the same loop it sells, run on its own funnel.

The numbers

The payment data is read from Stripe, and it has been steady rather than spectacular — which is its own kind of signal. In October 2026 traxy was at $52,700 in monthly recurring revenue, with $155,000 collected all-time and 240 subscriptions. The daily pulls through November moved in a narrow band: $54,400 on October 24, $52,700 on October 30, $54,100 on November 11, $56,000 on November 18, and $54,600 on November 20 and 21. As of November 21, 2026: $54,600 MRR, $48,900 in the last 30 days, $194,000 all-time, 240 subscriptions.

That flatness is worth stating plainly. This is not a hockey stick; it is a working business holding a level while its founder keeps building. The all-time figure grew from $155,000 to $194,000 in about three weeks, which is the number that shows the engine running. Retention, churn and customer-acquisition cost are undisclosed, and no founder-reported profit figure exists yet — the revenue is verified, the margins are not.

The next milestone is the same loop at larger scale: more markets watched every hour, more moments caught while they are warm. The agency taught him where the money is made — in the middle, in the minutes after someone engages. The product just makes sure nobody has to watch the feed by hand to be there for it.

The playbook

  1. Sell the service first, productize what works — Windmill proved the buyer-finding system on paying clients before it became software. A service that works is the cheapest validation a tool can get.
  2. Move on intent within moments — The whole product is one timing insight: a like or comment is warmest in the minutes after it happens. Build the workflow around the moment, not around the lead list.
  3. One plan, one job — A single $149 Pro plan for one job — find buyers already engaging. No tier maze, no feature table to argue about, just the job and the price.
  4. Build in public from day one — Day 32 of the series: 100 active users, 13 paying users, posted on LinkedIn. The build log is the distribution channel.
