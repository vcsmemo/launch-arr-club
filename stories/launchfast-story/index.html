How Launch Fast hit $30k MRR: the non-technical founder who shipped in 48 hours

Hasaam Bhatti had no CS degree and ten failed products. Then he gave himself 48 hours to build an Amazon seller tool — and hit $30k MRR.

Ten failures before the one that worked

Hasaam Bhatti's founder story starts with the least glamorous credential in tech: no computer science degree, never wrote a line of code. He worked a corporate job and ran two Amazon brands on the side — and it was the side hustle, not the day job, that made him a founder. He spent 20 to 30 hours researching every product idea, copy-pasting data into Google Sheets, frustrated by tooling that never quite fit how sellers actually work.

Before Launch Fast, there were ten or twelve attempts: AI video tools, job automation apps, a graveyard of projects that never made it to production. Looking back, Bhatti is blunt about why they died — he was building for audiences he didn't belong to. The products were guesses about strangers' problems. Amazon was different. He was the user, he knew exactly what was broken, and the domain knowledge was already in his head.

Then he found Cursor on Twitter. For a non-technical founder in the AI era, that discovery was the missing piece: the ideas and the domain expertise were ready, and suddenly the execution barrier had collapsed. All that was missing was a forcing function.

Forty-eight hours that changed everything

The forcing function came from an unlikely place: a coaching program called Legacy X that Bhatti had bought two years earlier. It had thousands of active Amazon sellers — his exact target users — already looking for better tools. So he made them an offer: give me 48 hours to build the solution. If you like it, we partner. No strings attached.

He didn't make that promise because he was confident. He made it because he needed a deadline real enough to override his planning instinct. The 48 hours were choreographed: the first four without touching Cursor at all, mapping the sellers' workflows, SOPs, and data. Hours five through twelve building core features in Cursor — functional, not perfect. Then testing, UI polish (branding matters, he'd learned from Amazon), edge-case testing, and finally a recorded demo video. That video was the pitch.

They called the next morning and told him to quit his job. Day 30, Launch Fast hit $10,000 in MRR. Day 60, $17–18k. Day 90, $21.8k. A few months later: $30k MRR — built entirely in Cursor by someone who, by his own admission, still can't read a stack trace without AI help.

No free tier, no vanity metrics

The growth engine behind Launch Fast is refreshingly unsexy. The first users didn't come from marketing; they came from the Legacy X partnership — equity traded for instant access to thousands of sellers. Bhatti is honest that this isn't a replicable hack for everyone, but it solved the hardest problem — distribution — on day one.

From there, the acquisition engine was built deliberately. Education became the core: weekly sessions teaching sellers not just the software but the Amazon business itself, because people who feel taught tend to stay. Technical blog posts compounded into Google traffic. Free tools served as top-of-funnel for sellers not ready to pay. Meta ads covered paid acquisition. But Bhatti insists the real driver is product quality — every meaningful improvement to the research engine shows up in referrals and retention, because sellers talk to each other constantly.

And critically: there is no free tier. Plans run $49, $89, and $199 a month, all paying users. That single decision shapes everything — no vanity metrics, no support burden from free riders, and every roadmap choice grounded in what paying customers actually need.

From seller tool to agentic platform

What's most interesting about Launch Fast in 2026 is where it's going. The team recently shipped an MCP server — one of the first all-in-one agentic tools for Amazon sellers — letting them research products, manage catalogs, and optimize ads through AI without bouncing between five tools. The agentic layer is still early, but it points at the bigger thesis: sellers don't want another dashboard, they want outcomes.

Bhatti has also started taking on contracts, helping other founders build products end to end, and running enterprise workshops on Claude Code and agentic workflows. The non-technical-founder-builds-SaaS-in-48-hours story opens doors, and he's walking through them — while staying close to the hard problems rather than the theoretical ones.

His advice to new founders distills the whole journey into three lines: build where you already have domain knowledge, solve distribution before anything else — even if it costs equity — and use a forcing function to ship before you feel ready. The tools to build without a CS degree exist now. The knowledge of what to build is already in your head. The only thing between you and shipping, he says, is the decision to stop planning and start.

The stack evolution mirrors the revenue: Cursor and Vercel and Supabase at the start, then Cloudflare and self-built crawlers once scale demanded control. Each infrastructure decision was pulled by real usage, never pushed by premature optimization — the indie hacker's version of just-in-time architecture.

The playbook

  1. Only build what you've suffered through — Ten failed products taught Bhatti the real lesson: build for a problem you've personally lived — 20-30 hours of spreadsheet research as an Amazon seller.
  2. Sell the demo before it exists — He promised a coaching program's sellers a working demo in 48 hours before writing a line of code — the deadline forced shipping over planning.
  3. Borrow someone else's distribution — Instead of hunting for users, he traded equity for access to thousands of sellers who needed the tool — distribution solved on day one.
  4. Charge from the first user — No free tier, ever. $49/$89/$199 plans mean every user is a paying user, and every feature decision is grounded in revenue.
  5. Evolve the stack with revenue — Cursor, Vercel, Supabase, then Cloudflare and self-built crawlers — start with managed services, rebuild only what success forces you to.
