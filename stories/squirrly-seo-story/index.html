He Shut Down 16,000 Websites, Read The Dip, and Has Been Shipping SEO Software Since 2012

Squirrly SEO founder Florin Muresan failed publicly with a 16,000-website community, raised a German angel round, and is still shipping SEO software more than a decade later — longevity as strategy.

16,000 Websites, Then a Shutdown

Before Squirrly, Florin Muresan's first startup was a website builder with a community attached. "It managed to gather a community of 16,000 websites," he said in a 2016 interview on his own site — "but in the end, it failed to be validated by the market, so we had to shut it down."

Sixteen thousand websites. That's not a side project; that's a real community, real usage, real momentum — and it still wasn't a business. Most founders bury a failure like that. Muresan talks about it, and cites the book that framed the decision for him: Seth Godin's "The Dip," about knowing when to quit versus when to persevere. He quit. That discipline — shutting down something with 16,000 users because the market wouldn't validate it — is rarer than any growth hack.

The stats on his bio from that era read like a serial builder's ledger: 13 SaaS products, 25,000+ paying businesses, 700,000+ leads, 29 software products. Squirrly was not his first swing. It was the swing after he'd learned what quitting costs and what it saves.

A German Angel Round and a London Start

According to the Squirrly SEO Plugin Media Kit from 2018: "2012. Squirrly SEO received an Angel Investment from two amazing investors in Germany, started the company in London, United Kingdom and the product launched in beta just a few days before Christmas."

The timeline matters. 2012 was early for WordPress SEO plugins as a venture-scale category — the space was dominated by free tools and hobby projects. Raising an angel round from German investors and incorporating in London signaled ambition beyond a plugin: this was meant to be a company.

And the earliest signal of product-market fit came from the users themselves: alpha testers, the media kit notes, "paid for Squirrly one year in advance." Before the beta, before the marketing, people were prepaying for a year of software they'd barely seen. That's the kind of validation 16,000 free websites never gave him — money, upfront, from strangers.

The Co-Founder He's Worked With for Years

"My favorite two mentors are my co-founder, Calin Vingan (we've worked together for about 8 years already…)" — Muresan, 2016. Calin Vingan is the CTO and co-founder of the company, and that parenthetical carries the whole story: by the time of the interview, the partnership was already eight years deep, stretching back before Squirrly to earlier ventures.

Founder mythology loves the lone genius. Muresan's version is a working partnership measured in nearly a decade. The mentor he names first isn't a famous investor or author — it's the person building the product next to him. That longevity in the founding team mirrors the longevity of the product: both are still going.

It's worth noting for the record: some sources blur the roles, but the company's own structure is clear — Florin Muresan is founder and CEO; Calin Vingan is CTO and co-founder. The company has since been renamed AISQ, reflecting a broadening beyond WordPress into AI search.

Customer Service Over Product Quality

Muresan's stated operating philosophy is a provocation to every product-led founder: "Excellence in Customer Service is much more important for me than excellence in product quality." Read it twice. He is not saying quality doesn't matter. He is saying that in a crowded market — and WordPress SEO plugins were brutally crowded — the support experience is the differentiator users actually feel.

This is the philosophy of someone who watched 16,000 websites evaporate. The product was good enough to attract a community; it wasn't a business because the market wouldn't pay. The lesson he took wasn't "build better" — it was "serve better." A plugin that answers your ticket today beats a marginally better plugin that answers next week, especially for the non-technical WordPress users Squirrly targets.

It's also a moat that compounds quietly. Product features get copied; a service reputation, built over a decade, doesn't.

The numbers behind the longevity

Muresan's 2016 bio reads like a ledger of persistence: 13 SaaS products, more than 25,000 paying businesses, 700,000+ leads generated, 29 software products shipped. Those are not the stats of a founder who found one idea and rode it — they are the stats of someone who treats shipping as the strategy itself. Squirrly SEO, launched in beta just before Christmas 2012, is simply the one that endured.

The media kit adds a detail that explains why: Squirrly's alpha testers paid for the product a full year in advance, before it was finished. And Muresan's reading of Seth Godin's "The Dip" — the book about knowing when to quit versus when to push through — frames the whole arc. The website builder with 16,000 sites was the dip he quit; Squirrly was the dip he pushed through. A decade later, the company has renamed itself AISQ and expanded from a WordPress plugin into AI marketing software covering AEO and GEO — still shipping, still customer-service-first.

Longevity as Strategy

Squirrly SEO launched in beta just before Christmas 2012. More than a decade later it's still shipping — now as AISQ, a WordPress SEO plugin plus cloud SaaS covering the new frontiers of AEO and GEO (answer-engine and generative-engine optimization), social, and email. The free tier is $0; PRO is $19.99 a month on annual billing.

Think about what that span covers: the plugin survived Panda and Penguin, the mobile shift, the Gutenberg editor rewrite, the rise of page builders, and now the AI-search upheaval that is rewriting what "SEO" even means. Most WordPress plugins from 2012 are abandonware. Squirrly renamed itself and kept building.

The arc from 16,000 dead websites to a decade-plus company is the whole story. Muresan learned to quit what the market wouldn't validate, took German angel money to start properly in London, kept the co-founder partnership for the long haul, bet on customer service over product perfection — and then simply refused to stop shipping. In software, longevity isn't luck. It's a strategy, executed for thirteen years.

The playbook

  1. Quit what the market won't validate — Shut down a 16,000-website community citing Seth Godin's "The Dip" — perseverance is only a virtue when the market is listening.
  2. Get paid before the beta — Alpha testers paid for Squirrly a year in advance — upfront money from strangers beats any amount of free-user enthusiasm.
  3. Keep the co-founder for the long haul — Eight years working with CTO/co-founder Calin Vingan by 2016; Muresan names him as his top mentor.
  4. Bet on service over product — "Excellence in Customer Service is much more important for me than excellence in product quality" — the stated differentiator in a crowded plugin market.
  5. Survive by renaming and rebuilding — From 2012 WordPress plugin to AISQ with AEO/GEO cloud SaaS — the company outlived its category by evolving with it.
