750 Conversations, Zero Outside Capital: How Celine Haaverstad and Chris Lee Bootstrapped SoftSync to Six-Figure MRR in Six Months

SoftSync's founders spent months listening to founders and operators before writing the product — then launched an AI CRM that runs itself, hit #1 on Product Hunt, and reached six-figure MRR in six months without raising a dollar.

750 conversations before a single line of positioning

Before SoftSync existed, its founders had about 750 conversations with founders and operators. The message came back the same way almost every time: people wanted AI working for them inside the business, not another tool to babysit. That sentence — from co-founder and CEO Celine Haaverstad, in the company's November 2026 announcement — is the whole product thesis. Most CRMs fail, in SoftSync's telling, because someone has to type everything in: meeting notes stay in a notebook, follow-ups slip, and the pipeline goes stale within a week.

The 750 conversations were not a validation exercise. They were the spec. SoftSync describes itself as “a CRM an AI assistant can actually run” — an all-in-one AI CRM built for founders, startups and small teams, where the everyday flow is outreach, meeting, proposal, closing, and the AI does the work in between. The founders did not start with a feature list copied from incumbents; they started with the complaint they heard 750 times and built the tool that makes the complaint go away.

A CRM an AI assistant can actually run

SoftSync has three parts that developers and operators tend to ask about, and co-founder Celine Haaverstad walked through all three in an October 2026 deep-dive. The Meeting Assistant is a desktop app for Mac and Windows that records meetings without ever joining the call as a bot — no awkward guest in the participant list, no “is it recording?” moment for the customer. Then there is the MCP server, which lets assistants like ChatGPT and Claude create customers, move deals and send email inside the CRM, within the permissions the team sets. And Autopilot, which turns routine work described in plain words into work that just happens.

The through-line is that nobody has to prompt, babysit or type to get value out of it. Data is hosted in the EU, on Azure in Stockholm, and the company says workspace data is never sold and never used to train models — a line that matters to the small businesses it sells to. Every plan carries the same features, including the AI assistant, Autopilot, the desktop recorder, the API and MCP; Pro starts at $29 per seat per month, billed yearly.

Bootstrapped to six figures in six months

SoftSync launched in May 2026 and stayed bootstrapped — no outside capital, profitable from early on, a Delaware C-corporation run from Wilmington. The launch was named #1 Product of the Day on Product Hunt, and it drew about 1.5 million views on X. That is a lot of attention for a CRM, a category not known for viral launches, and it says something about how hungry small teams are for the promise SoftSync makes: software that does the admin instead of creating more of it.

The founders are Celine Haaverstad and Chris Lee, the company's co-founders, with Lee as CTO. The six months from launch to the November milestone read like a compressed version of the usual SaaS story: no funding announcement, no hiring spree, just a product that found its users and kept them. By November the company served more than 500 paying customers — small businesses and sales teams, the exact audience from those 750 early conversations.

The growth, in numbers

The number the company put in its November 12, 2026 announcement is a range, not a point: six-figure monthly recurring revenue, reached within six months of the May launch, with revenue up 60% in October alone. Six figures means at least $100,000 a month — a $1.2 million annual run rate — from a product that charges $29 per seat per month and sells to small teams. The October jump is the interesting part: 60% in a single month, six months in, suggests the growth is compounding rather than flattening after the launch spike.

There is no outside funding to explain the curve, and no enterprise sales motion behind it either. The company says it is profitable, and the customer count — more than 500 paying — against the revenue range implies teams of several seats each, not a long tail of single-seat accounts. It is the shape of a product that small businesses adopt team-wide once one person tries it, which is exactly what a CRM that removes data entry should do.

The bet: software that does the admin

SoftSync's bet is that the next CRM will not be a better database with a nicer interface — it will be a colleague. The meeting recorder that never joins the call, the MCP server that lets an assistant do the work, the Autopilot that takes plain-word instructions: each piece assumes the user would rather not be in the tool at all. That is a different design instinct from the incumbents, which mostly assume a sales ops person will live inside them all day.

Whether the bet scales past six figures is the open question — the company is small, bootstrapped, and six months old. But the early evidence points the same way: 750 conversations that all said the same thing, a launch the market noticed, 500-plus paying customers, and a growth rate that accelerated in October instead of fading. For founders, the lesson is the boring one that keeps working: talk to an almost unreasonable number of users before you build, then build the thing they all asked for — and charge for it from the start.

The playbook

  1. Interview 750 users before you build — About 750 conversations with founders and operators before writing the product — the spec came from users, not from a competitor teardown.
  2. Build for the AI assistant, not the data-entry clerk — The Meeting Assistant records from the desktop and never joins the call as a bot; MCP lets Claude and ChatGPT work inside the CRM; Autopilot turns plain-word instructions into routine work.
  3. Remove work, don't reorganize it — No bot in the meeting, no prompting to get value, no data entry to keep the pipeline honest — the product removes work instead of reorganizing it.
  4. One price, all features — Every plan has the same features and Pro starts at $29 per seat per month — the company sells the product, not the tier maze.
  5. Stay bootstrapped and let customers fund growth — No outside capital, profitable from early on, and a launch that drew 1.5 million views on X — growth funded by customers, not investors.
